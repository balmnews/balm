King Charles Calls Britain's Slave Trade Role 'Shameful' but Stops Short of Formal Apology

King Charles, speaking at a Commonwealth heads of government meeting in Antigua, described Britain's role in the transatlantic slave trade as a 'shameful chapter of history' that fills him with 'horror' and 'profound personal sorrow,' but did not issue a formal apology or commit to reparations. King Charles addressed Commonwealth heads of government in Antigua, calling Britain's role in the transatlantic slave trade a 'shameful chapter of history' and stating that it fills him with 'horror' and 'the most profound personal sorrow.' He said that deepening his understanding of slavery had made him more determined to address the wrongs of the past, adding that 'we cannot change the actions of those who came before us.' However, the King stopped short of a formal apology on behalf of the British government or the Crown, and made no specific commitment to reparations. Caribbean leaders and campaigners had intensified calls for a formal apology and reparations payments ahead of the summit. The speech takes place against a backdrop of growing international pressure on former colonial powers to reckon with the economic and human legacy of the slave trade. The British government has not formally endorsed reparations, and the summit highlighted the tension between symbolic acknowledgment and legally or financially binding commitments. The Commonwealth, a grouping of 56 countries largely drawn from former British territories, includes many nations whose economies and societies were directly shaped by the slave trade.

High Diesel Prices Push Small Trucking Companies Toward Bankruptcy

Elevated diesel fuel costs are driving small trucking companies out of business, with firms operating on thin margins finding the price of fuel the difference between survival and closure. Small trucking companies across the United States are facing financial strain driven largely by sustained high diesel fuel prices. For operators with limited capital reserves and narrow profit margins — typical of small carriers — fuel costs represent a disproportionately large share of operating expenses. When those costs rise significantly, small firms have few levers to pull: they cannot easily renegotiate contracts, absorb losses over long periods, or hedge fuel costs the way larger companies do. The result has been a wave of bankruptcies and closures among small trucking operators. The trucking industry is a critical link in U.S. supply chains, moving the majority of goods that reach retailers and consumers. A contraction in trucking capacity can contribute to delivery delays and higher prices for transported goods. Diesel prices are influenced by global oil markets, domestic refining capacity, and federal fuel taxes.

Supreme Court Signals Support for Catholic Preschools Seeking Exemption from Anti-Discrimination Rules

The Supreme Court heard arguments Tuesday in a case involving Catholic preschools in Colorado that object to participating in a state-funded universal pre-K program due to requirements that they serve LGBTQ families. The court's conservative majority appeared inclined to side with the schools. The Supreme Court heard oral arguments Tuesday in a case that pits Colorado's universal pre-K program against Catholic preschools that say participating would violate their religious beliefs. Colorado's program provides publicly funded preschool access to families statewide, but requires participating providers to comply with the state's anti-discrimination law, which includes protections for LGBTQ families. The Catholic preschools challenging the program argue that being required to serve families whose relationships conflict with Catholic teaching violates their religious liberty under the First Amendment. Based on questions and comments from the justices during oral arguments, the court's conservative majority appeared to lean toward the schools' position. A ruling in favor of the schools could have significant implications for how states design publicly funded programs that involve religious institutions, potentially limiting the conditions governments can attach to funding when religious organizations are involved. The case is one of several this term testing the boundaries between anti-discrimination law and religious exemptions, an area where the court has consistently expanded religious liberty protections in recent years. A decision is expected by the end of the court's term next summer.

Two lots of a combination blood pressure medication containing olmesartan have been recalled nationwide due to concerns that the tablets may not dissolve properly in the body. Improper dissolution could reduce the medication's effectiveness, posing a risk to patients relying on it to control blood pressure. The recall applies to specific lot numbers of the three-drug olmesartan combination tablet. Patients who take olmesartan-based blood pressure medications are advised to check their bottles against the recalled lot numbers and contact their pharmacist or prescribing physician for guidance on replacement medication. Do not stop taking blood pressure medication without consulting a healthcare provider, as abrupt discontinuation can carry its own health risks.
