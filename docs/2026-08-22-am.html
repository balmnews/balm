Arizona faces the steepest cuts, with its share of the river shrinking by roughly a third, while some water policy experts say the reductions do not go far enough. The federal government has finalized a 10-year blueprint governing water use from the Colorado River, mandating cuts of up to 40 percent for the three states most dependent on the river's lower basin: Arizona, California, and Nevada. Arizona faces the sharpest reductions, with its allocation shrinking by approximately one-third, a cut that officials and analysts say will likely increase reliance on groundwater and place significant pressure on agriculture in the state. The plan comes after years of tense negotiations and missed deadlines, following the expiration of a previous governing framework. The Colorado River has been in a prolonged drought-driven decline for more than two decades, with reservoir levels at Lake Mead and Lake Powell — the two largest reservoirs in the US — having fallen to historic lows in recent years before partial recovery. Water policy experts quoted in reporting on the plan express concern that the mandated reductions, while significant, may not be sufficient to stabilize the river under current and projected climate conditions. The Guardian's reporting specifically notes that experts say the plan won't be enough to stop what they characterize as an ongoing water supply problem. The Colorado River is a water source for approximately 40 million people across seven US states and parts of Mexico, and it supports agricultural operations that produce a substantial share of the nation's vegetables and fruits. The new framework will shape farming decisions, municipal water planning, and energy production in the American West for the coming decade. Legal and political disputes among the affected states over the allocation of cuts are expected to continue.
