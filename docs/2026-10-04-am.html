Brazil held the first round of its presidential election on Sunday, with incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva facing challenger Flávio Bolsonaro in what polls described as a tight contest. Lula, running in his seventh presidential election overall, has strengthened Brazil's economic and diplomatic ties with China during his current term. Flávio Bolsonaro, son of former far-right President Jair Bolsonaro, has aligned himself with U.S. President Donald Trump and represents a conservative political movement that has been gaining ground across Latin America. A change in government could affect trade policy, environmental agreements — particularly around the Amazon — and the regional balance of power between Washington-aligned and Beijing-aligned governments. Supreme Court to Hear Case That Could Determine Oil Industry Liability for Climate Damages

The Supreme Court is preparing to hear a lawsuit that could determine whether oil companies face billions of dollars in liability for climate change damages — one of the most consequential environmental legal disputes in U.S. history. The Supreme Court is set to take up a lawsuit that could fundamentally shape the legal and financial exposure of the oil and gas industry for damages attributed to global warming. The case originates from a lawsuit filed by the city of Boulder, Colorado, and joins a broader wave of climate liability suits brought by states and municipalities against major fossil fuel companies. At issue is whether such cases can be heard in state courts — where plaintiffs may have more favorable legal terrain — or whether they must be adjudicated in federal court, where different standards apply. The outcome could affect not only the Boulder case but potentially hundreds of similar suits filed by cities and states across the country, collectively seeking billions of dollars in damages. The oil industry has argued that climate policy is a matter for Congress and federal regulators, not the courts. The case arrives at the court with its current conservative supermajority, making the ruling's direction uncertain but its impact potentially far-reaching for both the energy sector and local governments that have invested in climate litigation strategies.

Congress Votes to Close Legal Loophole That Allowed Hemp THC Products, Threatening $28 Billion Industry

Congress voted to eliminate a federal loophole that had allowed hemp-derived THC products to be sold legally across the U.S., potentially ending a $28 billion market. Congress has voted to close a loophole in federal law that has for years permitted the sale of hemp-derived products containing intoxicating levels of THC — the compound responsible for the psychoactive effects of cannabis. The loophole, created by language in the 2018 Farm Bill that legalized industrial hemp, was widely used by manufacturers to produce and sell gummies, beverages, and other consumer products that functioned similarly to marijuana but were technically legal at the federal level. The hemp THC market grew to an estimated $28 billion in annual sales. Under the new congressional action, those products would be classified as controlled substances, effectively shutting down the industry unless additional legislation is passed. Businesses in the sector are actively lobbying against the change, arguing that their products are distinct from marijuana and that the market supports hundreds of thousands of jobs. The vote represents one of the most significant federal interventions into the cannabis-adjacent marketplace in years and could affect consumers in states where marijuana itself remains illegal but hemp THC products have been freely available.

Targeted Cancer Drugs Show Strong Results but Reach Only a Fraction of Eligible Patients

New targeted gene therapies are proving highly effective against specific cancers, but cost, the sheer number of available treatments, and slow adoption are preventing most eligible patients from accessing them. A new generation of targeted cancer therapies — drugs designed to attack specific genetic mutations driving a patient's tumor — has demonstrated strong effectiveness in clinical settings. However, researchers and oncologists report that the treatments are reaching only a small fraction of the patients who might benefit. Multiple factors contribute to the access gap. The number of targeted therapies has grown rapidly, creating complexity for physicians who must identify which patients qualify through genetic testing. The cost of both the genomic screening and the drugs themselves remains high, limiting access for patients without comprehensive insurance. Additionally, medical practice changes slowly, and many community oncologists — who treat the majority of cancer patients — may not be fully current on the latest targeted options. The issue is not only a medical one but a health equity concern, as patients at large academic centers are more likely to be offered testing and enrollment in trials than those treated in smaller facilities. The article's framing is supported by specific cases, including the drug daraxonrasib, a treatment for pancreatic cancer that has shown promise but which patients eventually develop resistance to — a scientific challenge separate from the access problem.
