Supreme Court Allows Third-Country Deportations to Resume, Will Hear Full Case in December

The Supreme Court permitted the Trump administration to continue deporting migrants to countries not listed in their removal orders while the justices prepare to hear full arguments on the policy in December. The Supreme Court has allowed the Trump administration to resume deporting migrants to third countries — nations other than the migrants' countries of origin — while the broader legal challenge to the policy works its way through the courts. The justices granted the administration's request to lift a lower court order that had blocked the practice, meaning deportations can proceed in the near term. The court has also agreed to hear full oral arguments on the case in December, meaning the policy's long-term legal status remains unsettled. The practice of deporting migrants to third countries has been one of the administration's more contested immigration enforcement tools. Under the policy, individuals subject to removal orders may be sent to nations with which the United States has negotiated agreements, regardless of whether those countries are named in the individual's deportation paperwork. Immigrant rights advocates argued the lower court order should stay in place, contending the policy exposes migrants to potential harm without adequate legal process. The administration countered that the practice is a lawful exercise of executive authority over immigration enforcement. The December argument will give the court an opportunity to issue a definitive ruling on the policy's legality.

Senate Passes Bipartisan Bill to Restructure College Athletics Rules

The US Senate passed a bipartisan bill that would significantly restructure how college athletics are governed nationwide, including rules around athlete compensation and eligibility. The legislation now moves to the House. The US Senate has passed a major bipartisan bill that would overhaul the regulatory framework for college athletics across the country. The legislation addresses a range of longstanding issues in college sports, including athlete compensation, eligibility rules, and the governance structure that has governed the industry for decades. The bill passed with bipartisan support, reflecting broad agreement in the Senate that the current system — shaped largely by the NCAA and complicated by a series of court decisions and state laws on athlete name, image, and likeness rights — needs a unified federal framework. The bill now moves to the House of Representatives, where it faces a more uncertain path. House members are expected to debate provisions that affect universities, athletic conferences, and student athletes differently depending on their sport, size, and revenue.

Senate Rejects Democratic Resolution Seeking Report on West Bank Violence Against Americans

The US Senate voted largely along party lines to reject a Democratic resolution that would have required the State Department to report to Congress on violence in the West Bank, including the deaths of nine American citizens. The US Senate rejected a Democratic resolution that would have required the State Department to provide Congress with a formal report on violence in the occupied West Bank, including incidents resulting in the deaths of nine American citizens attributed to Israeli settlers or security forces over a period of years. The vote fell largely along party lines, with Republicans voting to block the measure. Proponents argued that Congress has a responsibility to track incidents in which American citizens are killed abroad and to hold the executive branch accountable for how it responds diplomatically. Opponents of the resolution argued it unfairly singled out Israel or was not an appropriate use of congressional oversight mechanisms. The vote reflects ongoing divisions in Congress over how to engage with US-Israel policy at a time when West Bank settlement activity and settler violence have drawn increased international attention.

White House Secures Voluntary AI Safety Commitments From Major Tech Companies

President Trump announced that leaders of major US technology and AI companies signed a voluntary agreement on frontier AI safety at a White House meeting, framing it as a self-policing commitment rather than a regulatory measure. President Trump announced a voluntary agreement called the Joint Commitment On Frontier Responsibilities, signed by the heads of major US technology and artificial intelligence companies following a White House meeting. The commitment outlines self-imposed safeguards on the development of advanced AI systems, though the specific measures are described in general terms and carry no legal enforcement mechanism. The president also used the occasion to encourage adoption of the term 'superintelligence' in place of 'artificial intelligence,' which he said he prefers. The agreement does not create binding regulatory requirements. AI safety researchers and some lawmakers have argued that voluntary industry commitments are insufficient to manage the risks posed by increasingly capable AI systems. The announcement came the same week that OpenAI separately scrapped the release of a new AI model after internal safety testing raised concerns, and as the company was facing congressional scrutiny over a separate incident involving its technology hacking Australian government systems.
