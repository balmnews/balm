Federal Judge Dismisses Trump's $10 Billion Defamation Lawsuit Against the Wall Street Journal

A federal judge dismissed President Trump's defamation lawsuit against the Wall Street Journal with prejudice, ruling that Trump did not demonstrate the newspaper acted with actual malice in its reporting on an alleged birthday letter to Jeffrey Epstein. U.S. District Judge Darrin Gayles in Miami dismissed President Donald Trump's $10 billion defamation lawsuit against the Wall Street Journal with prejudice on Saturday, meaning the case cannot be refiled. The lawsuit centered on the Journal's reporting about an alleged birthday letter Trump sent to Jeffrey Epstein. The judge ruled that Trump failed to meet the legal standard of demonstrating that the defendants made defamatory statements with actual malice — a high bar that applies to public figures under established First Amendment law. This was Trump's second attempt to pursue the claim; an earlier dismissal in the same matter had preceded this filing. A dismissal with prejudice is a final resolution on the merits, closing off further litigation on these specific claims in this court. The ruling is a significant outcome for press freedom advocates, who have watched a series of high-profile defamation suits brought by public figures against news organizations.

UN Biodiversity Summit Concludes Without Meeting Funding or Action Targets

The COP17 UN biodiversity summit concluded with member countries renewing commitments to halt nature loss but leaving key questions about financing unresolved, continuing a pattern of missed targets in the convention's 34-year history. The 17th UN Conference of the Parties on biodiversity, held in Yerevan, Armenia, concluded with countries renewing pledges to protect wildlife and restore ecosystems, but without agreement on how those efforts would be funded. The outcome extends a pattern: in the 34-year history of the Convention on Biological Diversity, governments have not met a single agreed target. Critics at the summit called for structural reform to the negotiating process, with some arguing that the current consensus-based system allows individual countries to block progress. Funding gaps between wealthy and lower-income nations remain the central unresolved tension. The summit's conclusion drew criticism from environmental advocates who said the text agreed upon does not translate into concrete, enforceable action. A BBC report described the talks as reaching a deal, while Guardian reporting was more critical of the outcome, reflecting genuine divergence in how observers assessed the final agreement. The next steps will depend on whether countries take independent domestic action in the absence of a binding international framework.

NASA's Swift Observatory is expected to re-enter Earth's atmosphere on or around Friday after a rescue mission failed to raise its orbit. Most of the 1.5-ton spacecraft is expected to burn up during re-entry. NASA's Swift Observatory, which has operated for 22 years studying powerful cosmic explosions, is expected to fall back into Earth's atmosphere on or around Friday after an attempt to boost its orbit failed. The U.S. Space Force is tracking the spacecraft's descent. The observatory weighs approximately 1.5 tons, but most of its mass is expected to burn up during atmospheric re-entry, reducing the risk of debris reaching the ground. The Swift telescope launched in 2004 and built a distinguished scientific record, detecting and studying gamma-ray bursts — among the most energetic events in the known universe — along with other high-energy phenomena. Rescue efforts were pursued to extend the mission but ultimately did not succeed in stabilizing the craft's orbit. Re-entry predictions carry inherent uncertainty, and the specific impact point, if any debris survives, cannot be confirmed far in advance. NASA has not issued a public safety warning related to the re-entry at this time.
