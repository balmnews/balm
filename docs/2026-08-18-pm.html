Lakes Mead and Powell Drop to Lowest Levels Since 1957, Threatening Southwest Water Supply

Water levels in Lakes Mead and Powell — the nation's two largest reservoirs — have fallen to their lowest point since 1957, worsening a water supply crisis affecting seven states dependent on the Colorado River. Water levels in Lake Mead and Lake Powell, the two largest reservoirs in the United States, have dropped to levels not recorded since 1957, according to federal data. Both reservoirs are formed by dams on the Colorado River and together supply water to seven western states: Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming, as well as parts of Mexico. The decline reflects years of drought, rising temperatures, and demand that has consistently exceeded the river's natural flow. Scientists studying the Colorado River basin have noted that climate change is reducing the snowpack in the Rocky Mountains that historically replenished the river each spring. Low reservoir levels threaten municipal water supplies for tens of millions of people, agricultural irrigation across a major food-producing region, and hydroelectric power generation at facilities including Hoover Dam. Federal and state water managers have implemented mandatory cuts in water allocations to some users in recent years, and further reductions may be required if levels continue to fall. The situation underscores a long-term structural imbalance between the water committed under century-old interstate compacts and the actual amount the Colorado River currently produces — a gap that regional officials, farmers, and federal agencies are under increasing pressure to resolve.
