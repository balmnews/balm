The State Department is assuming control of global health initiatives previously managed by the CDC, including large portions of US disease-monitoring and treatment work abroad. Critics say the department lacks the necessary public health expertise. A new plan would transfer significant control of US global health programs — including portions of the CDC's international disease work and oversight of PEPFAR, the HIV/AIDS treatment initiative — to the State Department. The restructuring reduces the CDC's direct role in managing health responses abroad. Public health experts and former officials have raised concerns that the State Department does not have the technical infrastructure or scientific expertise to manage programs that require specialized epidemiological knowledge. PEPFAR, established in 2003, has been credited with saving millions of lives across sub-Saharan Africa and other regions with high HIV burdens. The scale-back also follows broader budget and staffing cuts at the CDC and other federal health agencies. The reorganization has direct implications for American biosecurity interests, as international disease surveillance programs serve as an early-warning system for outbreaks that could eventually reach the United States.

A memo from the Justice Department has raised concern among disability rights advocates by challenging the legal framework that has long required the government to favor community-based care over institutionalization for people with disabilities. The protections in question stem largely from the Supreme Court's 1999 Olmstead decision, which held that unjustified institutionalization of people with disabilities constitutes discrimination under the Americans with Disabilities Act. The DOJ memo, according to advocates, signals a possible retreat from the department's historical role in enforcing those protections. Disability rights organizations warn the shift could expose more people to placement in institutional settings rather than supported community living arrangements, reversing decades of legal progress. The memo does not change existing law, but the Justice Department's enforcement posture significantly shapes how those laws are applied in practice.

The decision upheld a lower court ruling and sided with SpaceX and the Texas General Land Office. Environmental advocates had argued the closures violated the Texas Open Beaches Act, which traditionally guarantees public access to the state's shoreline. The court's decision was based on standing grounds rather than a ruling on the merits of the beach access argument.

Researchers Identify Two Biological Subtypes of Autism Using Brain Imaging

Scientists studying brain scans in both humans and mice have identified two distinct biological subtypes of autism — one linked to reduced brain connectivity related to synaptic function, and one linked to increased connectivity tied to immune system activity. A new study has identified two biologically distinct subtypes of autism spectrum disorder using brain imaging data from both mice and humans. Researchers found that one subtype is associated with reduced connectivity between brain regions, linked to differences in synaptic function — the mechanism by which neurons communicate. The second subtype shows increased connectivity, which the researchers connected to immune system activity in the brain. The distinction is significant because autism has historically been treated as a single diagnostic category despite wide variation in how the condition presents. Identifying biological subtypes could eventually support more tailored research into underlying causes and, in the longer term, inform the development of more targeted interventions. The study adds to a growing body of research using neuroimaging to move beyond behavioral classification toward biological understanding of neurodevelopmental conditions.
