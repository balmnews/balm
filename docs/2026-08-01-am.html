In late July, an estimated 60,000 migrants crossed from Morocco into Ceuta, a Spanish enclave on the northern tip of Africa, in a rapid mass movement over approximately 24 hours. At least 67 people died in the attempt, including some who drowned and others killed in a stampede at a breakwater barrier. The figures on the death toll vary slightly across sources, with an earlier count of 18 rising to 67 as the situation developed. Spain deployed military forces to the territory in response. Sánchez called this and similar moves by some EU states "selfish, polarizing, and unlawful." Spain has since installed a floating barrier in Ceuta. The incident carries direct relevance for the United States: the Trump administration commented publicly on the events, and the episode is being used in domestic American political debate about immigration policy heading into the midterm elections. The crossing represents one of the largest single migration events in recent European history and is accelerating debate about the EU's Schengen border framework.

The proposal would impose cuts of approximately 40% to the allocations of Arizona, California, and Nevada during dry years over the coming decade. Four other states that also draw water from the Colorado River — Colorado, Utah, Wyoming, and New Mexico — would not face mandatory cuts under the current proposal. The Colorado River supplies water to about 40 million people and irrigates millions of acres of farmland across the American West. Lake Mead and Lake Powell, the two largest reservoirs on the river, have faced historically low levels in recent years due to prolonged drought, increased demand, and the effects of a warming climate. The Bureau of Reclamation's plan is a federal proposal, not a final agreement. States and water agencies are expected to contest elements of the plan, and legal challenges are widely anticipated. Sources note that even this proposed fix may be temporary and would need to be revisited as conditions evolve. The plan comes after years of negotiations among the seven Colorado River basin states failed to produce a voluntary agreement on sharing cuts. The federal government's decision to publish its own framework represents a shift toward a more directive federal role in water allocation.
