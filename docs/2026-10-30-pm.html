New Study Estimates Three in Four Retired NFL Players Had CTE; Researchers Explore Possible Treatments

A new statistical study estimates that approximately three in four retired professional football players likely had chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, at death. Separately, researchers are exploring potential treatments including near-infrared light therapy. A newly published study using statistical modeling estimates that roughly three out of four retired NFL players most likely had chronic traumatic encephalopathy, known as CTE, at the time of their death. CTE is a degenerative brain disease associated with repeated head impacts and can only be definitively diagnosed after death. The estimate, if accurate, would represent a substantially higher prevalence than prior studies based on donated brain samples — which researchers acknowledge may be subject to selection bias, as families of players with symptoms are more likely to donate brains for study. In parallel research, scientists are exploring novel approaches to treat or potentially prevent CTE, with one of the more unexpected avenues being near-infrared light therapy, which some early studies suggest may help reduce brain inflammation. The NFL has been under sustained scientific and legal scrutiny over player brain health for more than a decade. These findings are expected to intensify ongoing debates about football's long-term safety and the sport's future at youth and professional levels.
