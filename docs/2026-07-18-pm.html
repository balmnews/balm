New Blood Test Can Predict Alzheimer's-Related Cognitive Decline Years Before Symptoms Appear

A blood test measuring a protein called p-tau217 identified symptom-free adults at high risk of cognitive decline up to ten years before expected onset, predicting a 78% likelihood of decline in those with very high biomarker levels, according to research findings. Researchers have developed a blood test using a biomarker called p-tau217 that can predict the likelihood of Alzheimer's-related cognitive decline in people who show no symptoms, potentially five to ten years before onset. In the study, adults with very high levels of the biomarker had a 78% predicted probability of developing cognitive decline within that window. The test could allow earlier intervention and potentially expand the pool of patients who qualify for emerging Alzheimer's treatments, which tend to be more effective when administered before significant brain damage occurs. Widespread clinical use would require further validation, regulatory review, and questions of accessibility and cost to be addressed. The development adds to a growing set of blood-based tools being investigated for early Alzheimer's detection, an area of active research given the limitations of current diagnostic methods.

Federal Government Ends Automatic Protections for Newly Listed Threatened Species

The U.S. government has eliminated the automatic legal protections that previously took effect when a species was added to the threatened species list, requiring instead that individualized protection plans be developed for each species before protections apply. The federal government has ended the longstanding policy of automatically extending legal protections to animals newly listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act. Under the previous framework, a species added to the threatened list received the same core protections as endangered species immediately upon listing. Under the new policy, each newly listed threatened species will require its own individualized protection plan before those safeguards take effect. Critics and conservation advocates have warned the change could leave vulnerable species exposed to habitat destruction or other harms during the period between listing and plan development, potentially accelerating the decline of some populations toward extinction. The Endangered Species Act, passed in 1973, currently protects over 1,600 species in the United States. The policy change affects the implementation of the law without requiring Congressional action.
