Major Housing Bill Becomes Law Without Trump's Signature

The 21st Century Road to Housing Act became law after President Trump declined to sign or veto it, allowing it to take effect automatically at midnight. The bipartisan measure is described as the most significant federal housing legislation in decades. A significant federal housing law took effect after President Trump allowed the 21st Century Road to Housing Act to become law without his signature, a procedural outcome that occurs when a president neither signs nor vetoes a bill within the constitutional timeframe. Trump stated publicly that he would not sign the measure because the Senate had not passed his preferred election legislation, known as the SAVE Act, but he also chose not to veto it. The bill passed Congress with bipartisan support. The law introduces a combination of incentives and regulatory changes aimed at increasing the US housing supply and improving affordability. It focuses primarily on encouraging local governments and homebuilders to make incremental changes, including streamlining permitting processes and providing financial incentives for new construction. Analysts describe it as meaningful but not transformative — notable progress within the constraints of what federal housing policy can directly accomplish. The US has faced a sustained housing shortage, with home prices and rents remaining elevated in most major markets. The new law does not impose direct rent controls or build housing itself, but rather creates conditions intended to encourage more private construction over time. The administration's decision to allow the bill to pass without a signature reflects an unusual approach — using the law as political leverage while declining to formally endorse or reject it.

Trump Administration Narrows Endangered Species Act Protections, Opening Habitats to Development

The Trump administration finalized a rule that redefines the term "harm" under the Endangered Species Act, effectively removing protections against habitat destruction for imperiled wildlife and opening those areas to logging, mining, and drilling. The Trump administration finalized a regulatory change to the Endangered Species Act on Friday that significantly narrows how the law protects imperiled wildlife. For roughly 50 years, the law's definition of "harm" included destroying the habitat that listed species depend on — meaning developers, logging operations, and extractive industries could face restrictions even if they did not directly kill protected animals. The new rule removes habitat destruction from that definition, meaning activities such as logging, mining, and drilling can proceed in areas used by endangered species without triggering the law's core protections. The Endangered Species Act, passed in 1973, is credited with preventing the extinction of roughly 99% of the species listed under it. Environmental groups have called the change a major setback for biodiversity, arguing that habitat loss is the leading driver of species decline. The administration framed the change as reducing regulatory burdens on land use industries. The finalized rule was announced by the Environmental Protection Agency. Legal challenges from conservation organizations are expected, as courts have previously considered habitat protection central to the law's intent. The change affects all currently listed threatened and endangered species in the United States, which number in the thousands.

New York State Sues 3M, DuPont and Others Over PFAS 'Forever Chemical' Contamination

New York State filed a lawsuit against 3M, DuPont, and other chemical companies, alleging they sold products containing PFAS chemicals while concealing known environmental and health risks from the public for decades. New York State sued multiple major chemical manufacturers, including 3M and DuPont, alleging that they caused a public nuisance by selling products containing per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances — known as PFAS or "forever chemicals" — while withholding information about the associated risks. The lawsuit claims the companies were aware of the environmental and health hazards posed by PFAS and chose to conceal this information even as they began phasing the chemicals out of their own product lines. PFAS chemicals are called "forever chemicals" because they do not break down naturally in the environment or in the human body. They are found in a wide range of consumer goods and have been linked to a variety of health conditions including certain cancers, immune system effects, and thyroid disruption. PFAS contamination has been detected in drinking water supplies across the United States. New York's suit adds to a growing body of state-level litigation against PFAS manufacturers. Several companies have previously reached large settlements with other states and municipalities over similar claims. The lawsuit seeks to hold manufacturers financially responsible for remediation costs and public health damages. 3M announced in 2022 that it would exit PFAS manufacturing, and DuPont has separately spun off its specialty chemicals operations, creating some complexity in assigning liability.

The Trump administration has petitioned the Supreme Court to rehear its birthright citizenship case. The Trump administration has formally asked the Supreme Court to rehear its case on birthright citizenship, a request that is uncommon in the Court's history. Rehearings — where the full Court reconsiders a decision it has already made — are granted in only a small fraction of cases. The administration's position has sought to narrow the application of the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship clause, which grants citizenship to all persons born on US soil. The Supreme Court addressed the issue during its most recent term. Cecillia Wang, the National Legal Director of the American Civil Liberties Union, argued the opposing position before the Court and participated in a recent public review of the term. She and other legal observers noted the significance of the case as a test of executive authority to reinterpret long-established constitutional provisions. The administration's petition for rehearing keeps the matter before the Court rather than allowing lower court rulings to govern enforcement. The legal question of birthright citizenship has substantial implications for immigration policy, as the administration's preferred interpretation would deny automatic citizenship to children born in the US to parents who are not citizens or lawful permanent residents.

US Weather Service Analysis Finds Historically Strong El Niño Increasingly Likely This Year

The US National Weather Service's latest models indicate a high probability that the current El Niño event will rank among the largest on record since 1950, with the potential to intensify extreme weather globally. Analysis from the US National Weather Service indicates that the El Niño weather pattern currently developing has a high likelihood of becoming one of the strongest on record, potentially ranking among the largest events going back to 1950. El Niño is a periodic warming of surface waters in the central and eastern Pacific Ocean that disrupts normal atmospheric circulation patterns worldwide. Strong El Niño events are associated with intensified droughts in some regions, increased flooding and precipitation in others, stronger hurricane seasons in certain basins, and broader disruptions to agricultural production. The 1997-1998 El Niño event — one of the strongest on record — caused tens of billions of dollars in damage globally and is considered a benchmark for the scale of impacts a major event can produce. For the United States, strong El Niño events typically bring wetter-than-average conditions to the southern US in winter and can suppress Atlantic hurricane activity while increasing Pacific storm systems. The current analysis raises the probability of significant weather disruption in the coming months across multiple regions.
