A federal judge has struck down an administration policy that halted the processing of immigrant visas for citizens of 75 countries. The policy, directed by the Secretary of State, denied visas to applicants from those nations on the basis that they were likely to require public assistance after arriving in the United States. The court ruled that the policy ran contrary to the Immigration and Nationality Act, which does not permit visa refusals to otherwise eligible applicants without a lawful basis. The judge found that the Secretary of State lacked the legal authority to impose the blanket suspension and that the policy illegally discriminated on the basis of nationality. The ruling vacates the policy entirely, meaning visa processing for affected applicants must resume under existing legal frameworks. The administration has not immediately indicated whether it will appeal. The case is the latest in a series of legal challenges to immigration enforcement actions taken by the current administration. Courts have previously blocked other immigration-related executive measures on similar grounds — that the actions exceeded statutory authority or conflicted with existing law. The 75-country list was broad and covered applicants from regions spanning multiple continents. For affected families and individuals who had pending visa applications, the ruling represents a significant legal development, though processing backlogs built up during the suspension period may take time to clear.

Federal Government Announces Colorado River Water Cuts for Arizona, California, and Nevada

The federal government has announced mandatory water cuts for Arizona, California, and Nevada under a new 10-year plan to manage the declining Colorado River. Arizona faces reductions of up to a third of its allocation. The US government has announced water allocation cuts for three states that draw from the Colorado River — Arizona, California, and Nevada — under a newly released 10-year management plan. The plan mandates reductions of up to 40% for the affected states, with Arizona facing the steepest cuts, potentially losing roughly a third of its current share. The Colorado River supplies water to approximately 40 million people across the American West and supports vast agricultural operations. The river has been under severe strain for decades due to prolonged drought, rising temperatures, and water usage that has consistently exceeded what the river can replenish. Reservoir levels at Lake Mead and Lake Powell — the two largest reservoirs on the river — have fallen to historically low levels in recent years. Arizona's reductions are expected to increase pressure on groundwater supplies and raise costs and constraints for agriculture in the state, which is one of the largest agricultural water users in the region. The plan covers California and Nevada as well, though the degree of cuts varies. Experts cited in coverage have noted that while the plan represents a meaningful step, the mandated reductions may be insufficient to stabilize the river system under projected climate conditions. Previous river management frameworks expired without replacement after years of negotiations among the seven states that share the Colorado River. The new plan provides a 10-year framework, but debates over its adequacy and implementation are expected to continue among water managers, farmers, tribes with treaty water rights, and municipal authorities across the region.

Wildfires in France's Gironde Region Burn More Land Than Any Previously Recorded Event

Fires in France this summer have burned more land than any previously recorded, displaced more people than at any point since World War II, and may have produced the country's first fire-generated thundercloud. Researchers trace contributing factors to a 19th-century decision to plant monoculture pine forests. Wildfires in France's Gironde region have, as of this summer, burned a larger area than any previously recorded fires in the country. The scale of displacement — forcing residents from their homes — has been the largest since the Second World War. Scientists believe the fires may also have generated what would be France's first documented firestorm thundercloud, a phenomenon in which fire activity is intense enough to produce its own weather system. Researchers and historians trace the current conditions to a combination of factors that have been building for more than a century and a half. An 1857 government decree drained coastal swamps and mandated the planting of monoculture maritime pine forests across the Landes region. Over generations, dense pine forests accumulated deep layers of dry needle debris — described as acting like explosive material in fire conditions. Combined with decades of warming temperatures that have increased drought stress and fire weather, the landscape became particularly susceptible. The fires have broader implications beyond France. The region's pine forests had served as a significant carbon sink, and their combustion releases stored carbon into the atmosphere. The scale and intensity of the fires also represent a reference point for what researchers project will be increasingly common fire events across Europe as temperatures continue to rise. Emergency response, land management practices, and forest composition policies are all under review in the wake of the fires.
