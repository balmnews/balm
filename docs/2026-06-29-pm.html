Supreme Court Expands Presidential Power to Remove Independent Agency Officials in 6-3 Ruling

The Supreme Court voted 6-3 to strike down a 91-year-old precedent that had shielded members of independent federal agencies from presidential removal, significantly expanding executive authority. In a separate order, the court temporarily blocked the removal of a Federal Reserve governor. The Supreme Court issued a landmark ruling allowing the president to fire commissioners of independent federal agencies, overturning a precedent that had stood since 1935. The 6-3 decision, split along ideological lines, eliminates the legal protection that had prevented presidents from removing members of agencies such as the Federal Trade Commission without cause. The ruling represents a major shift in the constitutional balance between the executive branch and regulatory bodies that were specifically designed to operate with a degree of independence from presidential control. The 1935 precedent, known as Humphrey's Executor, had been the primary legal barrier preventing such removals. The court's three liberal justices dissented, characterizing the ruling as destabilizing to the structure of independent oversight. In a related but separate action, the same court issued a temporary order blocking the Trump administration from removing Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook while her legal challenge proceeds through lower courts. The Fed case turns on whether the Federal Reserve's governance structure is legally distinct from the agencies covered by the main ruling; the court affirmed that Fed officials can be removed only for cause, though the administration indicated it would continue to pursue action against Cook. The dual decisions together clarify that while the president now has broad removal authority over most independent agency officials, the Federal Reserve retains some legal protection — at least for now. Legal analysts expect extensive further litigation as the lower courts work through cases involving specific agencies and officials.

Supreme Court Upholds State Laws Allowing Mail-In Ballots Received After Election Day

The Supreme Court ruled in favor of a Mississippi law permitting mail-in ballots postmarked by Election Day but received up to five days afterward to be counted, rejecting a Republican National Committee challenge. The Supreme Court upheld a Mississippi state law that allows election officials to count mail-in ballots that are postmarked by Election Day but arrive up to five days later. The ruling preserves similar grace period laws in more than a dozen states. The Republican National Committee had challenged the Mississippi law, arguing it created opportunities for fraud and undermined election integrity. The court sided against the RNC and the Trump administration, which had supported the challenge. Justice Samuel Alito filed a dissent, expressing concern that the ruling could undermine public confidence in elections. Following the decision, President Trump called on Congress to pass the SAVE Act, which would require voter identification and proof of citizenship. Supporters of the ruling argue that mail delivery delays are often outside voters' control and that postmark-based deadlines are a reasonable standard. The decision leaves intact the practice of grace periods in states that have enacted them, and does not require states without such laws to adopt them.

Supreme Court Rules Geofence Warrants Constitute an Unreasonable Search Under the Fourth Amendment

The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that law enforcement's use of geofence warrants — which compel tech companies to identify all devices in a given area at a given time — violates the Fourth Amendment's prohibition on unreasonable searches. The Supreme Court ruled that geofence warrants, a law enforcement technique that requires technology companies to provide data identifying all devices present within a defined geographic area during a specific time window, constitute a search under the Fourth Amendment. Writing for the six-justice majority, Justice Elena Kagan held that the practice is an unreasonable search and therefore requires more rigorous legal standards before law enforcement can obtain such data. Geofence warrants have been widely used in criminal investigations, including cases involving robberies and the January 6, 2021 Capitol breach, because they allow investigators to identify potential suspects even when the identity of the perpetrator is initially unknown. The ruling imposes new constitutional limits on this investigative tool and will require law enforcement agencies to meet a higher legal bar before using it. Three justices dissented. The decision has immediate implications for ongoing and future criminal cases in which geofence warrants were used to gather evidence.

Ebola Outbreak in Democratic Republic of Congo Could Become Largest on Record, Africa CDC Warns

Africa's Centers for Disease Control has warned that the current Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo could become the largest in recorded history, with most new cases appearing outside the surveillance network and contact tracing falling significantly behind. The Africa Centers for Disease Control has issued a warning that the ongoing Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo is on a trajectory that could make it the largest such outbreak ever recorded. The primary concern is that most people testing positive for the disease are not already known to health workers — meaning they have not been identified through contact tracing efforts and their exposure pathways are unknown. This pattern indicates that the virus is spreading through chains of transmission that public health authorities have not yet mapped, which is a significant indicator that containment is not keeping pace with spread. The current outbreak involves the Bundibugyo strain of the Ebola virus, which scientists believe persists in an animal reservoir that has not yet been identified. Periodic spillover from that unknown animal source into humans is thought to be the origin of outbreaks. The situation has direct relevance for global health surveillance, as the DRC has historically been the site of repeated Ebola outbreaks and is a major node in international travel and trade networks. The US government has in recent years contributed significantly to Ebola response and surveillance programs in the region.
