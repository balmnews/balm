Supreme Court Expands Presidential Firing Power Over Independent Agencies, With Federal Reserve as Exception

The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that presidents can dismiss leaders of independent federal agencies, overturning a 91-year precedent. In a separate ruling, the court found Trump's firing of Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook unconstitutional, protecting the Fed's independence. The Supreme Court issued a series of consequential rulings on Monday, substantially reshaping the balance of power between the presidency and federal institutions. In its most sweeping decision, the court voted 6-3 in Trump v. Slaughter to strike down a 91-year-old precedent that had protected members of independent regulatory agencies from removal by the president without cause. The ruling means the president now has broad authority to dismiss the heads of agencies such as the Federal Trade Commission and other commissions that were previously considered insulated from direct presidential control. Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Ketanji Brown Jackson, and Elena Kagan dissented. However, in a separate and significant ruling, the court found that the Federal Reserve occupies a distinct constitutional position and that President Trump's removal of Fed Governor Lisa Cook was unconstitutional. The court held that the president does not have authority to fire a Fed governor without cause while litigation over the dismissal remains active in lower courts, effectively allowing Cook to remain in her position for now. The court also ruled on mail-in ballots, upholding a Mississippi state law that allows ballots postmarked by Election Day to be counted if received within five days after the election. The Republican National Committee and the Trump administration had challenged the law, arguing it should be struck down. The ruling protects similar grace-period laws in more than a dozen states. In a fourth ruling, the court held that law enforcement's use of geofence warrants — broad requests for smartphone location data covering a geographic area — requires Fourth Amendment privacy protections. Justice Elena Kagan wrote that majority opinion. Taken together, Monday's decisions represent one of the most consequential single-day outputs from the court in recent memory, touching executive power, central bank independence, election administration, and digital privacy.

Supreme Court Upholds State Laws Allowing Mail-In Ballots Received After Election Day

The Supreme Court upheld a Mississippi law permitting mail-in ballots postmarked by Election Day to be counted if received within five days afterward, rejecting a challenge from the Republican National Committee and the Trump administration. The Supreme Court ruled Monday in favor of states that allow mail-in ballots to arrive after Election Day and still be counted, provided the ballots are postmarked by Election Day itself. The case centered on a Mississippi state law that gives election officials up to five days after the election to receive such ballots. The Republican National Committee and the Trump administration had challenged the law. The court's decision protects similar laws currently in effect in more than half of US states and the District of Columbia. The ruling was decided against the position of national Republicans, who have argued that counting ballots received after Election Day undermines election integrity. Supporters of the laws argued that postmark-based deadlines are a reasonable accommodation for voters relying on the mail and that disenfranchising voters for postal delays outside their control is unfair.

This gap suggests transmission is occurring in ways that current surveillance is not capturing.
