Supreme Court Allows Trump Administration to Proceed with Mail-In Voting Restrictions Before Midterms

The Supreme Court lifted a lower court block on the Trump administration's mail-in voting restrictions, allowing enforcement to proceed in 23 states ahead of November midterms, though U.S. Postal Service implementation remains separately blocked. The Supreme Court issued an unsigned order allowing the Trump administration to move forward with new restrictions on mail-in voting, pausing a lower court ruling that had blocked key parts of the policy. The decision clears the path for enforcement in 23 states ahead of November's midterm elections, though a separate block on U.S. Postal Service implementation of the order remains in place nationwide. The court's order was not accompanied by a full written opinion. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson wrote a dissent, arguing the ruling introduces uncertainty into the upcoming elections at a late stage. The ideological split among justices was apparent, though the majority's reasoning was not publicly detailed. Practical implementation faces significant time pressure. Some states are scheduled to begin mailing ballots to voters within weeks, leaving a narrow window for any meaningful changes to take effect. The Postal Service had outlined an implementation plan last week, but that portion of the order remains under a separate court-ordered hold. The administration's original executive order sought to tighten the conditions under which mail ballots could be requested, distributed, or counted. Courts at the district level had blocked portions of the order on constitutional grounds. The Supreme Court's intervention is not a final ruling on the merits — it allows enforcement to continue while legal challenges proceed through the courts. The decision is likely to affect voter access in states that had expanded mail-in voting in recent election cycles. Legal challenges are ongoing, and further court rulings before November are possible. Election administrators in affected states are now navigating uncertainty over which rules will apply.

Trump Administration Moves Toward Largest Mass Visa Revocation in U.S. History

The Trump administration is preparing to revoke visas for up to 200,000 foreign nationals who have applied for or are currently seeking asylum status in the United States. Separately, a federal judge vacated a related visa freeze covering 75 countries. The Trump administration is preparing what officials describe as the largest mass visa revocation in American history, targeting up to 200,000 foreign nationals who have applied for or are currently in the process of seeking asylum in the United States. The action would affect individuals who entered the country on nonimmigrant visas and subsequently filed asylum claims. The planned revocation is separate from an earlier executive order by Secretary of State Marco Rubio that imposed a visa freeze on nationals from 75 countries. A federal judge vacated that freeze, ruling against the administration's legal basis for the measure. State Department officials publicly criticized the ruling, characterizing the judge as acting outside appropriate judicial boundaries. The administration has not set a specific public date for the mass revocation, but officials indicated the process is in an advanced stage of preparation. The legal authority and procedural mechanisms for revoking visas at this scale are expected to face court challenges. These immigration enforcement actions are taking place alongside a broader pattern of measures including an ICE operation in Virginia and Maryland that resulted in 1,328 arrests over a 14-day period. The administration has described these as part of a sustained enforcement campaign rather than isolated operations. The visa revocation plan would have significant implications for immigration courts, which are already operating under a substantial backlog. Affected individuals would likely have limited time to respond or seek legal relief.

Nevada Sues Trump Administration Over Colorado River Water Use Cuts

Nevada filed a federal lawsuit challenging the Trump administration's Colorado River management plan, which would reduce water allocations to California, Arizona, and Nevada by roughly 20% over two years. The river supplies water to 40 million people across the American West. Nevada filed a federal lawsuit seeking to block the Trump administration's new management plan for the Colorado River, which would reduce water allocations to California, Arizona, and Nevada by approximately 20% over a two-year period. Nevada is the first state to sue over the plan, but officials and legal observers expect additional lawsuits from other affected states and stakeholders. The Colorado River is the primary water source for 40 million people, dozens of tribal nations, and approximately 5.5 million acres of farmland across the American West and Southwest. The river has been under severe stress for decades due to drought, overuse, and rising temperatures, with reservoir levels at major storage facilities remaining well below historical norms. The Trump administration's plan represents one approach to bringing water consumption into balance with actual river flows, which have declined significantly due to climate-related changes in precipitation and snowpack. States and water agencies have long disagreed over how to apportion the necessary reductions. Nevada's lawsuit argues the administration's plan exceeds federal authority or violates existing legal agreements governing water allocation. The case enters a complex legal landscape shaped by interstate compacts, tribal water rights, and decades of court rulings. The outcome will have direct consequences for municipal water supplies, agriculture, and economic activity across a region that has experienced rapid population growth in recent decades. Legal proceedings are expected to continue for months or years.

Researchers and Patients Challenge Whether FDA-Approved Cancer Drug Doses Are Too High

Researchers and patients are raising questions about whether FDA-approved dosages for certain cancer drugs are higher than necessary, with some arguing that lower doses could reduce side effects without compromising effectiveness. A growing body of research and patient advocacy is challenging the standard practice of approving maximum tolerated doses for cancer drugs, with some economists and medical researchers arguing that many FDA-approved dosages may be higher than required for therapeutic effect. The concern is that patients receive doses calibrated to the edge of tolerability, producing severe side effects, when lower doses might achieve similar outcomes with fewer harms. Northwestern University economist Chuck Manski is among those who have examined the issue. The argument centers on the clinical trial design process: early-phase cancer trials typically identify the highest dose patients can endure, which then becomes the approved dose. Critics argue this approach prioritizes maximum biological effect over patient quality of life. Patients who have experienced significant side effects from standard dosing have joined researchers in calling for trials that systematically test lower doses. Some oncologists already adjust doses in practice for individual patients, but formal lower-dose approvals are rare. The FDA has in recent years signaled openness to so-called dose optimization studies, but the pace of change has been gradual.
