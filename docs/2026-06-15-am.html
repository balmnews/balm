Trump confirmed the signing publicly but said the full text would be released shortly, describing it as a significant document. Reports indicate concern in Jerusalem about the deal's ambiguous terms and its effects on Israeli national security interests. In Kuwait and other parts of the region, the announcement was met with relief tempered by doubt about the deal's long-term durability. Iranian state media characterized the agreement as a victory. A formal signing ceremony was expected later in the week, though the deal's inherent ambiguities — particularly on nuclear matters — leave significant uncertainty about what further negotiations will produce. Analysts noted that both sides essentially returned to the position they held before the conflict began, at the cost of thousands of lives.

State PFAS Bans Have Reduced Forever Chemicals in Clothing, Though Some Companies Remain Non-Compliant

A new report finds that state laws banning PFAS chemicals in clothing and textiles have significantly reduced their presence in tested products, with about 80% of 115 items meeting compliance standards. Some companies continue to exceed legal limits. State-level laws banning per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances — commonly called PFAS or forever chemicals — from clothing and textiles have produced measurable results, according to a new report. Testing of 115 products found that approximately 80 percent had PFAS levels that complied with state regulations, a finding public health advocates describe as evidence that protective policy can drive industry change. However, the report also found that some companies appear to have ignored the new rules, with a portion of tested products still exceeding legal PFAS limits. PFAS compounds are called forever chemicals because they do not break down in the environment or in the human body, and have been linked to a range of health effects. The findings are relevant at the federal level, where the Environmental Protection Agency's approach to PFAS regulation has been under review. The patchwork of state bans has effectively acted as a national floor for some manufacturers.

Some parents and child advocacy organizations back the restrictions. Technology companies warned the ban could push younger users toward less regulated and potentially more harmful platforms. Privacy advocates raised concerns about the age verification methods that would be required to enforce the policy. The UK government's approach is described as going further than Australia's existing restrictions.
