Climate Models Indicate 'Super El Niño' May Bring Flooding to Western and Southeastern U.S. This Winter

Climate scientists say a developing El Niño system, intensified by above-average sea surface temperatures, is expected to bring a wet winter and potential flooding to portions of the western and southeastern United States, persisting into early spring 2027. Climate scientists are tracking a developing El Niño weather system that models suggest could intensify significantly in the fall and winter months, bringing substantially above-average precipitation and flood risk to portions of the western United States and the southeastern U.S., with effects potentially persisting into early spring 2027. El Niño is a recurring climate pattern driven by warmer-than-average sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific, which alters atmospheric circulation and shifts precipitation patterns across North America and globally. The system being forecast has been characterized by some researchers as a potential super El Niño, a designation reflecting unusual intensity. Coastal areas in the western U.S. are among those identified as potentially most exposed to flooding risk. The forecast adds to concerns already present after a winter that produced record-low snowpack in the Colorado River Basin, contributing to Lake Mead reaching historic low water levels. Residents and emergency managers in affected regions typically use El Niño forecasts to prepare infrastructure and emergency response resources in advance of wetter seasons.

Lake Mead, the largest reservoir in the United States, has dropped to a historic low water level. The decline follows a winter that produced the worst recorded snowpack in the Colorado River Basin. Lake Mead, the nation's largest reservoir by volume and a primary water source for millions of people across Nevada, Arizona, California, and parts of Mexico, has fallen to its lowest recorded water level. The record follows an exceptionally dry winter that produced the worst snowpack on record in the Colorado River Basin, which feeds the reservoir. Lake Mead's levels are a closely watched indicator of water supply across the American West, where prolonged drought and increased demand have strained the Colorado River system for years. Low reservoir levels trigger legally binding water allocation reductions affecting agriculture, municipal water supplies, and hydroelectric power generation. Federal and state water managers have been operating under drought contingency plans, but the new record low is expected to prompt further discussions about long-term water management and allocation agreements among the basin's seven states.
