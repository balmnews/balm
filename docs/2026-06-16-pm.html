Trump Administration Moves Special Education and Civil Rights Oversight Out of the Department of Education

The Trump administration is transferring civil rights enforcement and special education oversight from the Department of Education to the Justice Department and the Department of Health and Human Services, respectively — continuing a broader effort to dismantle the agency. The Trump administration announced it is shifting two major functions out of the Department of Education: civil rights enforcement will move to the Department of Justice, and oversight of special education programs will transfer to the Department of Health and Human Services. The moves represent a further step toward the president's stated goal of closing the Department of Education entirely. The civil rights office within the Department of Education has historically handled complaints related to discrimination in schools, including cases involving students with disabilities and those from minority groups. Advocates for students with disabilities have expressed concern that splitting oversight across agencies could reduce coordination and accountability. Congress created the Department of Education in 1979, and formally closing it would require legislative action. The administration is restructuring its functions through executive action in the interim. Critics, including some education policy organizations, argue the changes will create bureaucratic confusion and reduce protections for vulnerable students. The administration has characterized the moves as increasing efficiency.

Israel, shipping companies, and regional actors are navigating significant uncertainty about the deal's terms and durability. The United States and Iran have reached an initial agreement — described as a memorandum of understanding — covering the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and the outlines of a nuclear framework. A formal signing ceremony is scheduled for Friday, after which a 60-day negotiation window will begin to work out details including sanctions relief and Iran's nuclear concessions. President Trump described the agreement as a step toward normalizing relations, though officials from both governments acknowledged that core issues remain unresolved. Despite the agreement, shipping has not yet returned to normal through the Strait. Experts and industry leaders cite three primary obstacles: residual security concerns, the possible presence of naval mines on the sea floor, and questions about transit fees. The head of one of the world's largest tanker operators indicated that ships would need proof the agreement is functioning before resuming normal routes, and the US Navy is deploying drone-based systems to sweep for mines. The process could take weeks. The deal has introduced visible strain between the United States and Israel. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who entered the conflict alongside the US, waited more than a day before publicly responding to the agreement. He has not endorsed it, and Israeli defense officials say the Israeli military will remain in its current positions in Lebanon regardless of the deal's terms. Iran's foreign minister has separately stated that any broader peace arrangement must include Israeli withdrawal from Lebanon — a condition Israel has not accepted. Iran's state media characterized the agreement as a political victory, while analysts noted that for ordinary Iranians, the primary concern is whether it will reduce the cost of living and reduce the risk of further military conflict. Senate Republicans blocked a Democratic war powers resolution that would have constrained the administration's military authority during negotiations. The agreement's economic ripple effects are already being felt. Brent crude fell below $80 per barrel for the first time in more than three months following news that Iranian oil tankers had resumed some shipments. However, the cost of motor oil and lubricants — which depend on supply chains disrupted by the conflict — is expected to remain elevated even after the Strait fully reopens, as the inputs involved are not quickly replaceable. American farmers are also facing higher fertilizer costs linked to wartime supply disruptions.
