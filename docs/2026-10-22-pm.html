UN Nature Advisors Classify Nearly All Shallow Coral Reefs as Critically Endangered

The International Union for Conservation of Nature has classified almost all of the world's shallow-water coral reef ecosystems as critically endangered, with assessors projecting a high risk of collapse in more than 80 countries before 2075 if current warming trends continue. The International Union for Conservation of Nature has updated its Red List to classify nearly all shallow-water coral reef ecosystems on Earth as critically endangered — the highest risk category before extinction. The assessment, conducted by advisors to the United Nations, covers coral reefs across more than 80 countries and projects that approximately 96 percent of shallow reef ecosystems could collapse before 2075 under current climate trajectories. Among the reefs facing high collapse risk is Australia's Great Barrier Reef, the world's largest coral structure and a UNESCO World Heritage site. About 24 percent of the planet's reefs are already classified as being at critical risk under the assessment's criteria. Shallow coral reefs are among the most biodiverse marine ecosystems on Earth, supporting an estimated 25 percent of all marine species and providing food, coastal protection, and economic livelihoods — including through fisheries and tourism — to hundreds of millions of people globally. Ocean warming causes coral bleaching events, during which corals expel the algae that sustain them. Repeated or prolonged bleaching leads to mass die-offs. Sources reviewed for this story are consistent on the core findings, though they differ slightly in emphasis on the role of emissions reductions in altering the projected outcomes.
