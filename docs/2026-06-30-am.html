Supreme Court Expands Presidential Removal Power, Rules Against Trump on Three Other Cases

The Supreme Court ruled the president can fire commissioners of independent regulatory agencies, overturning 90 years of precedent. In the same session, the Court ruled against Trump on three other matters, including protecting Federal Reserve independence. The Supreme Court issued a set of consequential rulings in what turned out to be a significant day for the separation of powers. The most sweeping decision held that the president has the authority to remove commissioners of independent federal agencies — overturning a precedent that had stood for roughly 90 years. The ruling arose from a challenge involving a Federal Trade Commission commissioner and gives the executive branch substantially broader control over the leadership of dozens of regulatory bodies, including the FTC, the SEC, and other independent commissions. However, the same session produced three rulings that went against the administration. In a 5-4 decision, the Court blocked President Trump from firing Lisa Cook, a member of the Federal Reserve Board of Governors, ruling that the Fed occupies a uniquely protected status as a central bank and that the broader removal-power ruling does not extend to it. The Federal Reserve's political independence was thus reaffirmed even as the power over most other agencies expanded. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote a concurrence in the FTC case signaling his view that the ruling could open the door to further challenges against the structural independence of federal agencies, suggesting Monday's decision may be the beginning rather than the end of this line of legal development. The rulings carry practical consequences across the economy. Independent agencies oversee financial markets, consumer protection, telecommunications, and labor relations, among other areas. Legal analysts expect the removal-power ruling to face challenges and refinement in lower courts as affected agencies and their commissioners assess their new exposure.

The Supreme Court ruled that the president has the authority to fire commissioners of the Federal Trade Commission without cause, overturning a foundational precedent that had protected the independence of federal regulatory agencies since 1935. The case centered on whether statutory protections limiting the removal of FTC commissioners to cases of "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance" were constitutional. The Court held they were not, giving the executive branch direct control over an agency that oversees antitrust enforcement and consumer protection. Supreme Court Rules Trump Cannot Fire Federal Reserve Governor, Protecting Central Bank's Independence

In a 5-4 ruling, the Supreme Court blocked President Trump from removing Federal Reserve Board Governor Lisa Cook, finding the Fed's unique status as a central bank places it outside the broader presidential removal authority the Court expanded the same day. The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that President Trump does not have the authority to remove Lisa Cook from her position as a Federal Reserve Board of Governors member, in a decision that carves out a protected status for the central bank even as the Court simultaneously expanded presidential firing power over other independent agencies. Cook's legal team argued, and a majority of justices agreed, that the Federal Reserve's role as the nation's central bank — with direct responsibility for monetary policy and financial stability — makes it constitutionally distinct from regulatory commissions like the FTC. The ruling preserves the Fed's political independence at a moment when the administration had been seeking broader control over federal institutions. The decision has immediate relevance for financial markets, which closely monitor any threats to the Fed's independence as a guarantor of stable monetary policy.

Supreme Court Rules Fourth Amendment Applies to Cellphone Location Data Obtained via Geofence Warrants

The Supreme Court held that law enforcement use of geofence warrants — which pull location data for all devices in a given area — constitutes a search under the Fourth Amendment, requiring judicial oversight. The Supreme Court ruled that the government's use of geofence warrants, which compel technology companies to hand over location data for every device present in a defined geographic area during a specified time window, is a "search" under the Fourth Amendment. The ruling means law enforcement agencies must demonstrate probable cause and obtain judicial approval before using the technique. Geofence warrants have become a common investigative tool, used in cases ranging from robbery to the January 6 Capitol breach, and have drawn criticism from civil liberties advocates who argue they sweep up data from people with no connection to any crime. The decision sets a constitutional floor for how such warrants must be handled going forward, though the Court did not rule on whether any specific prior use was unlawful.
