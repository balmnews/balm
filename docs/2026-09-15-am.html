U.S. Military Confirms It Has Deployed Weapons in Space

The U.S. Air Force Secretary publicly confirmed for the first time that the United States has active weapons deployed in Earth's orbit, a disclosure analysts say could accelerate military competition with Russia and China in space. The United States has weapons deployed in space, Air Force Secretary Troy Meink confirmed in a public statement — the first time any U.S. official has publicly acknowledged the existence of American on-orbit weapons systems. The disclosure marks a significant shift in how the U.S. government communicates about its space-based military capabilities, which have long been classified. Analysts described the announcement as potentially consequential, noting that public confirmation of such capabilities could intensify competition with Russia and China, both of which have been developing their own space-based military assets. The Pentagon has for years been preparing for the possibility of conflict extending into orbit, viewing space as a domain increasingly central to military operations, communications, and surveillance. Neither the type of weapons nor their specific capabilities were disclosed. The announcement raises questions about international frameworks governing military activity in space, including the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which prohibits weapons of mass destruction in orbit but does not ban all weapons. The move comes amid broader tensions with both Russia and China over space policy, satellite operations, and the potential militarization of low Earth orbit.

Separately, reporting indicates the Houthis have built a sanctions-evasion network that includes Iranian oil sales, cryptocurrency, and Russia-linked financing to fund their military operations. Oil markets have already been under pressure due to the broader conflict involving Iran, and analysts warn the latest developments could further tighten global supply. The U.S. military has been conducting operations targeting Houthi positions, but the group continues to expand its operational reach across the region.

EPA Moves to Remove All Emission Limits on Coal and Gas Power Plants

The EPA announced it will repeal regulations that set carbon pollution limits on coal and natural gas power plants, removing the federal government's primary legal tool for reducing greenhouse gas emissions from the electricity sector. The agency projected up to $670 billion in savings for power plants; environmental groups called the move a significant setback for climate policy. The Environmental Protection Agency announced plans to repeal regulations that established carbon pollution standards for coal and natural gas power plants, effectively removing the federal government's principal mechanism for reducing greenhouse gas emissions from electricity generation. The agency framed the move as relieving financial burdens on power producers, projecting savings of up to $670 billion for the sector. The regulations being repealed were put in place under the Biden administration and represented the most significant federal effort to reduce power sector emissions in recent years. Environmental experts described the rollback as stripping the EPA of its ability to regulate greenhouse gases from power plants under the Clean Air Act, a legal authority the agency had used as the basis for the rules. The announcement came during what meteorological data indicate was the hottest summer on record in the United States. The repeal will face legal challenges, as previous attempts to limit or expand EPA authority over power plant emissions have been litigated extensively in federal courts, including a major Supreme Court ruling in 2022 that constrained the agency's regulatory reach in this area. The practical effect on emissions will depend on whether states or courts impose any constraints in the interim.
