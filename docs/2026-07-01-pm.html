Supreme Court Upholds Birthright Citizenship, Closing Out Its Term

The Supreme Court ruled that birthright citizenship is constitutionally guaranteed, affirming a right established in an 1898 precedent. The ruling came on the final day of the court's term, alongside decisions on transgender athletes in sports and campaign finance law. The Supreme Court upheld birthright citizenship on the final day of its 2025–26 term, ruling that children born on American soil are citizens regardless of their parents' immigration status. The court's majority described the ruling as breaking no new constitutional ground, reaffirming a precedent set in Wong v. United States in 1898. The decision directly blocks an executive order from the Trump administration that sought to restrict birthright citizenship to children of citizens or permanent residents. The ruling is among the most significant of the term on immigration law and drew immediate reactions across the political spectrum. On the same day, the court also issued decisions on transgender athletes' participation in sports and struck down a campaign finance law, making it one of the term's most consequential closing days. In Congress, lawmakers began discussing legislative responses to the birthright citizenship ruling, including proposals targeting what critics call birth tourism — the practice of traveling to the United States specifically to give birth. The Justice Department separately filed lawsuits against California and Virginia over state gun restrictions, citing a Second Amendment ruling the court also issued near the close of its term. The court additionally agreed to hear a new case in its next term on whether the Second Amendment protects possession of semiautomatic rifles, setting up a major firearms decision for 2026–27.

Several States Sue Federal Government Over New Medicaid Work Requirements

A group of states filed a lawsuit challenging new federal Medicaid work requirements, arguing the rules exceed what Congress authorized and contradict guidance the federal government previously provided to states. Multiple states have filed a legal challenge against the Trump administration's new Medicaid work requirements, contending that the federal government overstepped its authority in implementing the rules. The lawsuit argues that the requirements go beyond what Congress enacted when it created the Medicaid program and that they depart from federal guidance states had previously relied on in designing their Medicaid programs. Work requirements for Medicaid beneficiaries have been a long-standing policy goal of Republican administrations and have faced repeated legal challenges. Under the new rules, certain Medicaid recipients would need to document employment, job training, or community service activities to maintain eligibility. Critics argue this would result in eligible individuals losing coverage not because they fail to meet the work requirement itself, but due to administrative barriers such as paperwork burdens. The administration has argued that the requirements promote self-sufficiency and align Medicaid more closely with other federal assistance programs. The legal outcome could affect Medicaid coverage for millions of Americans, as the program serves roughly 80 million people nationwide.

Nuclear Regulatory Commission Plans to Overhaul Radiation Safety Standards to Expand Nuclear Power

The Nuclear Regulatory Commission plans to eliminate a longstanding principle requiring radiation exposure to be kept as low as reasonably achievable, as part of a broader effort by the Trump administration to accelerate nuclear energy expansion. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission is planning to end decades-old guidance that radiation exposure from nuclear facilities be kept as low as reasonably achievable — a principle known as ALARA that has underpinned US radiation safety rules since the 1970s. The planned overhaul is part of a broader push by the Trump administration to reduce regulatory barriers to nuclear energy development. Proponents argue that current safety standards are overly conservative and slow the construction and licensing of new reactors at a time when demand for low-carbon electricity is increasing. Critics, including some health physicists and environmental groups, contend that loosening radiation standards introduces risk and that the ALARA principle exists precisely because the health effects of low-level radiation exposure are difficult to measure but potentially cumulative. The NRC change would need to go through a formal rulemaking process before taking effect. Nuclear energy currently provides about 18 percent of US electricity. The administration has also taken separate steps to streamline reactor permitting and encourage development of advanced and small modular reactor designs.

US Blocks Long-Term Renewal of North American Trade Agreement, Triggering Annual Reviews

The United States has blocked a 16-year renewal of the North American trade deal covering the US, Canada, and Mexico, instead pushing for annual rolling reviews of the agreement. Negotiations to renew the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement have stalled after the US declined to accept a long-term extension, triggering a framework under which the deal would instead be subject to annual reviews. The original USMCA, which replaced NAFTA in 2020, contains a built-in review clause that was designed to be used under extraordinary circumstances. The US position shifts the trade relationship between the three countries onto less predictable footing, introducing uncertainty for businesses that rely on integrated North American supply chains. Canada and Mexico had sought a stable long-term renewal to provide investment certainty for industries including auto manufacturing, agriculture, and energy. The annual review structure means the terms of the agreement could be revisited — and potentially altered — each year, giving the US leverage in ongoing disputes over tariffs and trade practices. The USMCA governs roughly 1.3 trillion dollars in annual trade between the three countries. Business groups on all sides have expressed concern that the shift could discourage long-term cross-border investment.

Medicare Launches $50 Monthly Weight-Loss Drug Program; Eligibility and Concerns Outlined

Medicare has introduced a pilot program providing GLP-1 weight-loss drugs for $50 per month. The program has specific eligibility requirements, and health policy analysts have raised questions about cost sustainability and long-term coverage implications. Medicare has launched a pilot program that offers GLP-1 medications — the class of drugs that includes semaglutide, sold under brand names such as Ozempic and Wegovy — specifically for weight loss at a cost of $50 per month for eligible enrollees. Previously, Medicare covered GLP-1 drugs only for diabetes management, not for obesity treatment alone. The pilot program represents a significant policy shift that could expand access to a category of medication that has shown substantial effectiveness in clinical trials for weight loss. Eligibility is limited and tied to specific criteria related to body mass index and related health conditions. Health policy researchers have raised several concerns, including the long-term cost to the Medicare program if coverage were to be broadly expanded, given that GLP-1 drugs currently carry list prices of more than $1,000 per month before negotiation. Questions also exist about what happens to participants if they can no longer afford the medication once the pilot ends, as studies have shown weight typically returns after stopping the drugs. The pilot is being closely watched as a potential model for broader federal coverage decisions.

While the current outbreak is centered in the DRC, the Africa CDC warning is intended to raise regional and global preparedness.
