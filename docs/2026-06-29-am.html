Supreme Court Expands Presidential Firing Power Over Independent Agencies, With Federal Reserve Exception

The Supreme Court voted 6-3 to overturn a 91-year-old precedent, giving presidents broad authority to remove heads of independent federal agencies. The Federal Reserve was exempted, with the court blocking Trump's attempt to fire Fed Governor Lisa Cook while her legal challenge proceeds. The Supreme Court issued a landmark 6-3 ruling that significantly expands the president's power to remove officials from federal agencies previously considered independent of White House control. The decision overturns Humphrey's Executor, a 1935 precedent that had long protected the heads of agencies such as the Federal Trade Commission from being dismissed without cause. The ruling upheld President Trump's firing of an FTC commissioner, a decision with broad implications for the structure of the federal regulatory system. The Federal Reserve was explicitly carved out as an exception. In a separate order, the court blocked Trump's attempt to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook, allowing her to remain in her position while her legal challenge works through the lower courts. Following that ruling, Trump renewed his stated intention to take action against Cook, whom he had sought to dismiss citing allegations related to mortgage documents — allegations Cook has disputed, saying the removal attempt was retaliatory. Every living former Federal Reserve chair and a group of former Treasury secretaries had filed an unusual amicus brief arguing that the president's removal power over the Fed threatened economic stability. The three liberal justices dissented, with the minority calling the broader ruling destabilizing. The decision represents one of the most significant shifts in the balance of power between the executive branch and independent regulatory agencies in decades. Legal and economic analysts expect continued litigation over which agencies fall under the new framework and how far the ruling's reach extends.

Supreme Court Upholds State Laws Allowing Mail-In Ballots Received After Election Day

The Supreme Court ruled that states may count mail-in ballots postmarked by Election Day but received up to several days later, rejecting a Republican-led challenge to a Mississippi law and preserving similar rules in more than a dozen other states. The Supreme Court has upheld a Mississippi state law permitting election officials to count mail-in ballots that are postmarked by Election Day but arrive up to five days afterward. The ruling rejected a challenge brought by the Republican National Committee and supported by the Trump administration. The decision has national significance because similar grace-period rules are in effect in more than half of U.S. states and the District of Columbia. Had the court ruled the other way, it would have invalidated those laws and potentially disenfranchised voters whose ballots were mailed on time but delayed in transit. The ruling preserves the current electoral landscape for mail-in voting ahead of the 2026 midterm elections. The Republican challenge had argued that counting ballots arriving after Election Day violated federal law establishing a single election day; the court disagreed. The decision was not accompanied by a written majority opinion in the descriptions available, and the full reasoning will be important for understanding its scope.

Supreme Court Rules Geofence Warrants Require Fourth Amendment Protections

The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that geofence warrants — which collect smartphone location data from all devices in a given area — constitute a search under the Fourth Amendment and require constitutional privacy protections, limiting law enforcement's use of the technique. The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that law enforcement's use of geofence warrants violates the Fourth Amendment's prohibition against unreasonable searches. Geofence warrants allow investigators to request data from technology companies — typically Google — identifying all devices located within a geographic area during a specified time window, then narrow the pool to identify suspects. Justice Elena Kagan wrote the majority opinion, finding that the broad, indiscriminate sweep of such warrants amounts to a constitutionally regulated search. Critics of the technique had argued it functions as a dragnet that captures the location data of many innocent people alongside any potential suspects. The ruling will require law enforcement agencies to meet Fourth Amendment standards — including obtaining a warrant based on probable cause — before using geofence techniques. The decision affects a tool that has been used in a range of criminal investigations across the country and establishes a significant new boundary on digital surveillance practices.
