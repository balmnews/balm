Genetically Modified Pig Kidney Keeps Patient Alive for Nine Months Before Human Transplant

A genetically modified pig kidney kept a 66-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease alive for a record nine months, successfully functioning as a bridge until a human organ became available. A 66-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease caused by type 2 diabetes received a genetically modified kidney from a pig and survived on that organ for nine months — a world record for a xenotransplant of this kind. The pig kidney, from an animal named Wilma, functioned as a bridge to a subsequent human kidney transplant, which the patient, Tim Andrews, ultimately received. The case represents a significant milestone in xenotransplantation, the use of animal organs in human patients, which researchers have pursued as a potential solution to the chronic shortage of donor organs. Earlier pig kidney transplants in human patients had lasted days to weeks; nine months of sustained function in a living patient marks a meaningful advance. The genetically modified pigs used in these procedures have had certain animal genes removed and human genes introduced to reduce the risk of rejection. Andrews's case is described as the first in which such an organ successfully served as a planned intermediate step before a human transplant.
