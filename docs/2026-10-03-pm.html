Hemp THC Industry Faces Potential Federal Ban After Congressional Vote to Close Legal Loophole

Congress voted to close a legal loophole that has allowed a $28 billion market for hemp-derived THC products to operate, potentially eliminating the industry under federal law. The United States' multibillion-dollar market for hemp-derived THC products — including drinks, gummies, and other consumables sold legally in many states — faces potential elimination after Congress voted to close a loophole in federal law that had allowed it to flourish. The market, estimated at $28 billion, developed after the 2018 Farm Bill legalized hemp and its derivatives, with manufacturers exploiting the distinction between hemp-derived THC and marijuana-derived THC to sell products with psychoactive effects. Congress has now moved to close that gap, which would subject hemp THC products to the same federal restrictions as marijuana. The industry has been lobbying aggressively to preserve its legal status, arguing the products are distinct from marijuana and that thousands of small businesses depend on the market. If signed into law, the change would represent a significant federal intervention into a rapidly growing consumer products sector.

US Midwest Farmers Face Record Diesel Costs During Harvest Season

Farmers across the US Midwest corn belt are confronting sharply higher diesel costs this harvest season, with some reporting record fuel bills that are compressing already tight margins. Farmers in the US Midwest are entering harvest season facing record diesel expenses, adding significant financial pressure to an industry already dealing with elevated input costs. One Indiana corn and soybean farmer described an anticipated bill of roughly 9,000 gallons of diesel to power four tractors, two combine harvesters, and five semi-trucks through the harvest period. At current prices, that represents a record outlay. The surge in diesel costs follows global supply pressures and domestic policy uncertainty around fuel exports. Corn and soybean operations in states like Indiana, Illinois, and Iowa rely heavily on diesel-powered equipment during the narrow harvest window, when delays can result in significant crop losses. Higher fuel costs that cannot be passed on to buyers directly reduce farm income. The situation comes as Midwest agricultural communities are also grappling with trade policy uncertainty affecting commodity export markets.

The US Food and Drug Administration has classified its recall of Salata's Jalapeño Avocado Dressing to Class I — the agency's highest health risk designation, indicating a reasonable probability that consuming the product could cause serious harm or death.
