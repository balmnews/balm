Nobel Prize in Chemistry Awarded for Work on Mirror-Image Molecules

Henri B. Kagan of France and Kensō Soai of Japan have been awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for discoveries related to asymmetric synthesis and autocatalysis — research that illuminates why biological molecules favor one mirror-image form over another. The 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry has been awarded to Henri B. Kagan of Paris-Sud University in France and Kensō Soai of the Tokyo University of Science in Japan. The prize recognizes their work on how chemical reactions can produce an overwhelming excess of one of two mirror-image forms of a molecule — a phenomenon fundamental to life itself, since biological molecules such as amino acids and sugars are almost exclusively one-handed in nature. Kagan's work demonstrated non-linear effects in asymmetric synthesis, showing that small imbalances in a catalyst could be amplified dramatically. Soai discovered autocatalysis in asymmetric reactions, meaning a molecule can catalyze its own production in a self-amplifying process. Together, their findings have deepened scientific understanding of how biological asymmetry may have originated on Earth and have practical applications in pharmaceutical development, where the two mirror-image versions of the same molecule can have very different effects in the human body.

Nobel Prize in Physics Awarded to Researcher Behind South Pole Neutrino Detector

Francis Halzen, a Belgian physicist at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, has been awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work developing the IceCube detector at the South Pole, which identifies neutrinos arriving from deep space. The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics has been awarded to Francis Halzen, a Belgian particle physicist based at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, for his role in conceiving and building the IceCube Neutrino Observatory at the South Pole. IceCube is a cubic-kilometer detector embedded in Antarctic ice that identifies neutrinos — nearly massless, electrically neutral particles that travel across the universe at close to the speed of light and pass through ordinary matter almost without interaction. By detecting the faint flashes of light produced when neutrinos occasionally collide with ice molecules, IceCube has opened a new window on the high-energy universe, identifying sources of cosmic rays and advancing understanding of astrophysical phenomena that cannot be observed using conventional telescopes. The observatory represents a landmark example of large-scale international scientific collaboration.
