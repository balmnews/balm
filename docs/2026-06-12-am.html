Key U.S. Surveillance Authority FISA Section 702 Set to Lapse as Congress Fails to Renew It

FISA Section 702, a surveillance authority the government says underpins more than 60 percent of the president's daily intelligence briefing, was set to expire Friday after Congress failed to pass a renewal. Lawmakers have struggled to reach agreement on the tool's scope and oversight. A significant U.S. intelligence-gathering authority known as FISA Section 702 was set to lapse on Friday after Congress did not pass legislation to renew it. The government has stated that the tool accounts for more than 60 percent of the content in the president's daily intelligence briefing, making it one of the most widely used surveillance mechanisms available to American intelligence agencies. Section 702 allows the government to collect communications of foreign targets located outside the United States without an individual warrant, though the data collected often includes communications involving American citizens. The authority has long been subject to debate over privacy concerns and the scope of domestic surveillance it enables. Congress has repeatedly struggled to agree on the terms of renewal, with disagreements centering on oversight requirements, minimization procedures, and the conditions under which data on U.S. persons can be searched. The lapse does not immediately shut down existing collection, but it creates legal uncertainty about ongoing programs and complicates the initiation of new surveillance activities. The expiration represents a significant moment for U.S. intelligence capabilities and for congressional oversight of national security programs. Intelligence officials have consistently described Section 702 as essential to counterterrorism, counterintelligence, and other national security missions.

NOAA Declares El Niño Has Arrived; Scientists Say It Could Be Strongest of the Century

U.S. weather officials at NOAA have confirmed that El Niño has formed in the Pacific Ocean and predict it could intensify to historically strong levels by fall, potentially driving extreme weather events worldwide. The United Nations secretary-general described it as an urgent climate warning. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration confirmed that El Niño has officially arrived, and meteorologists are predicting the climate pattern could reach historically strong intensity by the fall — potentially the strongest El Niño of the current century. The United Nations secretary-general called the development an urgent climate warning. El Niño is a periodic warming of surface temperatures in the central and eastern Pacific Ocean that disrupts weather patterns globally. Strong El Niño events are associated with intensified droughts, flooding, heat waves, and hurricane activity in different parts of the world, and they tend to push global average temperatures higher. The 2015-2016 El Niño, which was among the strongest on record, contributed to record global temperatures that year. Scientists say the current pattern is forecast to intensify through summer and fall. For the United States, strong El Niño events have historically been associated with wetter conditions across the southern states in winter, drier conditions in the Pacific Northwest, and more active weather across the southern tier generally. The development comes as global temperatures have already been elevated, which could amplify El Niño's effects beyond historical norms.

Antarctic Sea Ice in Bellingshausen Sea Missing an Area the Size of France

Scientists report that Antarctica's west coast is missing an expanse of winter sea ice roughly the size of France in the Bellingshausen Sea, with temperatures in the region running approximately 20 degrees Celsius above average. Researchers say the failure of ice to form raises concerns for marine ecosystems and global sea levels. Researchers monitoring Antarctic sea ice say the Bellingshausen Sea, off Antarctica's west coast, is missing a vast area of winter sea ice that should have formed by this point in the season — a gap scientists describe as approximately the size of France. Temperatures in the region have been running about 20 degrees Celsius above the seasonal average, which scientists say has contributed to the failure of ice to form at expected levels. One expert described the situation as depressing and said the absence of ice could have intensified a regional heat wave, creating a feedback loop in which less ice leads to warmer temperatures that further inhibit ice formation. The Bellingshausen Sea has been one of the most rapidly changing areas of Antarctica in recent years. The consequences extend beyond the immediate region. Sea ice plays a role in regulating ocean temperatures and supports ecosystems that include penguin species already under pressure from climate change. The loss also has implications for global sea levels: while floating sea ice does not directly raise sea levels when it melts, reduced sea ice can accelerate the warming of underlying ocean waters, which in turn affects the land-based ice sheets whose melting does contribute to sea level rise.

Solar Power Surpassed Coal in U.S. Electricity Generation for First Time in May

Solar energy supplied 12.8 percent of U.S. electricity in May, exceeding coal-fired generation for the first time on record, according to data from the energy research group Ember. Solar power provided 12.8 percent of U.S. electricity generation in May, surpassing coal for the first time in recorded history, according to data released by Ember, a global energy research organization. The milestone comes even as the Trump administration has taken steps to promote coal production and roll back policies that favor renewable energy. Solar remains the leading source of newly installed electricity generation capacity in the United States, with installations continuing to expand despite federal policy headwinds. The May figure reflects both the growth in installed solar capacity and the seasonal advantage solar panels have during months with longer daylight hours, meaning the comparison with coal may narrow again in winter months. Coal's share of U.S. electricity generation has been declining steadily for over a decade, driven by competition from cheaper natural gas and, more recently, from rapidly falling solar costs. The new data point marks a symbolic threshold in that long-term shift, though natural gas continues to dominate overall U.S. electricity production by a wide margin.
