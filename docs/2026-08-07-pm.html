The Federal Reserve's independence from executive branch pressure is considered a cornerstone of monetary policy credibility, and investors and economists have been monitoring the dispute closely. A Supreme Court ruling on the underlying constitutional question could have lasting implications for the structure of independent federal agencies.

U.S. Economy Lost 23,000 Jobs in July as Prior Months Were Also Revised Down

U.S. employers shed 23,000 jobs in July, and job gains for the previous two months were revised down by a combined 103,000, presenting a weaker picture of the labor market than previously reported. The unemployment rate held steady at 4.1%. The Labor Department reported Friday that U.S. employers cut a net 23,000 jobs in July, a result that fell well below economist expectations of roughly flat or positive job growth. Compounding the weaker-than-expected headline number, job gains for May and June were revised downward by a combined 103,000 positions, suggesting the labor market has been softer for longer than prior data indicated. The unemployment rate remained at 4.1%, unchanged from the previous month. The report arrives at a sensitive moment for monetary policy. Federal Reserve officials have been weighing when to begin raising interest rates, with markets increasingly pricing in a potential rate increase as soon as September. Some analysts noted that a weak jobs report does not necessarily foreclose a rate rise, because the Fed's current focus is primarily on the trajectory of inflation, which has remained above the central bank's 2% target for several years. The report also arrives as Kevin Warsh, the new Fed chair, faces his first major policy test. Sources diverge somewhat on emphasis: while some highlight the report as a meaningful deterioration in labor market conditions, others stress that a single month's data and an unchanged unemployment rate leave the Fed's rate path uncertain. The figures will factor into the Fed's September meeting deliberations.

Global Food Prices Reach Highest Level in Three and a Half Years

The UN Food and Agriculture Organization's food commodity price index rose last month to its highest point since January 2023, driven by summer heatwaves and ongoing conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East pushing up crop costs. Global food commodity prices climbed to their highest level in three and a half years in July, according to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization's monthly price index. The FAO attributed the rise to a combination of factors: summer heatwaves that have reduced crop yields in key agricultural regions, and disruptions to supply chains and growing areas tied to conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East. The increase comes on top of already-elevated grocery prices in the United States, where food costs have been a persistent source of inflationary pressure. Higher global commodity prices typically flow through to consumer prices over subsequent months, affecting staple foods including grains, cooking oils, and dairy products. The trend is particularly significant for lower-income households, which spend a higher share of their budgets on food.

Louisiana health officials have reported at least five deaths this year from Vibrio vulnificus, a flesh-eating bacterial infection associated with contact with warm seawater or consumption of raw shellfish. Louisiana health authorities have reported at least five deaths in 2026 attributed to Vibrio vulnificus, a naturally occurring bacterium found in warm coastal waters that can cause severe, rapidly progressing infections in people who swim in affected water or eat raw shellfish, particularly oysters. The bacterium can enter the body through open wounds or cuts, and infections can progress quickly to life-threatening conditions. People with liver disease, diabetes, or weakened immune systems are at significantly higher risk of serious outcomes. Health officials advise avoiding saltwater exposure if you have open wounds, and cooking shellfish thoroughly. The reported deaths follow previous years in which warming Gulf Coast water temperatures have been associated with increases in Vibrio vulnificus cases.
