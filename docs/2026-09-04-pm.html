UN General Assembly Adopts Resolution to Phase Out Mercator Map in Favor of Equal Earth Projection

The UN General Assembly voted 164 to 1 to adopt a resolution phasing out the centuries-old Mercator map in favor of the Equal Earth projection, which more accurately represents countries' relative sizes. The United States cast the sole opposing vote. The United Nations General Assembly voted to adopt a resolution formally retiring the Mercator map — in use since the 16th century — in favor of the Equal Earth projection, which displays countries at their accurate relative sizes. The resolution, sponsored by Togo, was approved by 164 nations. The United States was the only country to vote against it. The Mercator projection, developed by Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator in 1569 primarily for maritime navigation, distorts the size of landmasses at higher latitudes. This has the effect of making Europe and North America appear substantially larger relative to equatorial regions, including Africa, than they actually are. Africa, for example, is approximately 14 times the land area of Greenland, yet on Mercator maps the two appear roughly similar in size. The Equal Earth projection, developed in 2018, uses mathematical techniques to preserve relative area while keeping the map rectangular and visually coherent. Proponents of the change argue that the distortions in the Mercator map have had educational and geopolitical consequences by understating the geographic scale of Africa and other regions in the Global South. The resolution calls for a phased transition in official UN materials and encourages member states to adopt the Equal Earth projection in educational settings. Its practical implementation will depend on how individual countries respond.

A genetically modified pig kidney kept a 66-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease alive for nine months — a world record — before he successfully received a human donor kidney. A 66-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease caused by type 2 diabetes survived for nine months with a functioning genetically modified pig kidney before receiving a human transplant, setting a new longevity record for xenotransplantation — the use of animal organs in humans. The pig kidney, from an animal named Wilma, was used as a bridge to keep the patient, identified as Tim Andrews, alive while a suitable human donor organ was located. The case represents a significant advance in xenotransplantation research, which has been pursued for decades as a potential solution to the chronic shortage of human donor organs. Tens of thousands of people in the United States are on waiting lists for kidney transplants at any given time, and many die before a matching organ becomes available. The nine-month survival with the pig kidney more than doubled previous records for this type of procedure and demonstrates that genetically modified animal organs may be viable as temporary or potentially permanent solutions. The genetic modifications to the pig are designed to reduce the human immune system's tendency to reject animal tissue. Researchers cautioned that while the case is promising, broader application will require further study.
