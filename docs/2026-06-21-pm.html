US and Iran Hold First Direct Nuclear Talks in Switzerland as Contradictory Signals Complicate Negotiations

Vice President JD Vance met with senior Iranian officials in Switzerland to begin a new round of nuclear negotiations, while President Trump simultaneously issued threats against Iran on social media, prompting Iranian negotiators to temporarily withdraw from direct talks. The United States and Iran opened a new round of nuclear negotiations at a Swiss mountain resort near Lake Lucerne on Sunday, with Vice President JD Vance leading the American delegation alongside envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. Iran's parliamentary speaker Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi represented Tehran. Qatar and Pakistan served as mediators. The talks aim to convert a recently signed interim memorandum of understanding into a durable agreement addressing Iran's nuclear program and formally ending the war in Iran. Iran's delegation was described as unusually broad, including its central bank governor and oil officials — a composition analysts interpreted as signaling Iranian priorities around sanctions relief and economic access. The first day was complicated by a stream of social media posts from President Trump, who threatened further military strikes against Iran and warned of consequences if Tehran did not restrain Hezbollah in Lebanon. Iranian negotiators walked out of direct talks in protest, leaving Qatar and Pakistan to continue shuttling between delegations. Vance publicly described progress as positive, calling the talks a step toward peace, even as the administration's public messaging remained divided. Adding to the complexity, fighting between Israel and Hezbollah in Lebanon continued to flare, with Iran insisting that an end to the Lebanon conflict is a precondition for substantive progress. There are also reported internal tensions within the Iranian government, with a former member of Iran's negotiating team facing potential prosecution after disclosing what he described as confidential correspondence from Iran's supreme leader. The talks carry significant economic weight. Oil markets have remained unsettled, with prices rising modestly on uncertainty over the Strait of Hormuz, through which a substantial share of global oil exports pass. The prospect of reopening the strait has not immediately altered purchasing patterns for major buyers such as China, which accumulated oil reserves during the conflict period. The Trump administration's provisional deal with Iran has drawn criticism from both parties in the United States. Some Republican lawmakers argue the deal concedes too much leverage; others in the president's orbit have supported the diplomatic approach. The next round of negotiations is expected to determine whether the interim agreement can be formalized.
