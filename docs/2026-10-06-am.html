Francis Halzen Awarded Nobel Prize in Physics for IceCube Neutrino Observatory at South Pole

Belgian-American physicist Francis Halzen has been awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for developing the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, a particle detector built into a cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice that captures high-energy neutrinos arriving from deep space. Francis Halzen, an 82-year-old Belgian particle physicist affiliated with the University of Wisconsin-Madison, has been awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for his pioneering work on the IceCube Neutrino Observatory at the South Pole. Neutrinos are subatomic particles that travel across vast cosmic distances largely unimpeded, passing through ordinary matter almost entirely undetected — which makes them exceptionally difficult to study but potentially rich sources of information about the universe's most energetic processes. Halzen's central contribution was the concept and development of IceCube, which transforms a cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice into a detection medium: when a neutrino occasionally interacts with the ice, it produces a faint flash of blue light that the detector's sensors can record. Halzen began developing the theoretical groundwork for this approach in the 1980s, and the completed IceCube detector was formally inaugurated in 2010. The observatory has since detected high-energy neutrinos arriving from outside our galaxy, opening what scientists describe as a new form of astronomy — observing the universe through particles rather than light. Halzen is the sole recipient of this year's physics prize. The Nobel Committee noted that his work represents a decades-long effort that transformed a speculative idea into a functioning scientific instrument producing landmark results.

2026 Nobel Prize in Medicine Awarded for Optogenetics — a Tool That Uses Light to Control Brain Cells

Three scientists — Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann, and Georg Nagel — have been awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for developing optogenetics, a technique that uses pulses of light to activate or silence specific neurons in living brains. The Nobel Assembly has awarded the 2026 prize in Physiology or Medicine to Karl Deisseroth of Stanford University and the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, Peter Hegemann of Humboldt University in Berlin, and Georg Nagel of the University of Würzburg, for their work on light-gated ion channels and the development of optogenetics. The technique at the heart of the prize allows scientists to introduce light-sensitive proteins, derived originally from algae, into specific neurons in the brain. When a pulse of light is then directed at those cells, they can be switched on or off with millisecond precision — a level of control that was previously impossible. This capability has allowed researchers to map the brain's circuitry with far greater specificity than earlier methods permitted, and has advanced understanding of conditions including depression, Parkinson's disease, and addiction. Hegemann and Nagel identified and characterized the light-sensitive proteins — known as channelrhodopsins — that made the technique biologically possible. Deisseroth's laboratory was instrumental in developing the method into a practical tool usable in intact living tissue and eventually in living animals. Since its development in the early 2000s, optogenetics has become one of the most widely used techniques in neuroscience research worldwide. The prize is awarded annually by the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm.
