US and Iran Sign Preliminary Agreement to End Conflict and Reopen Strait of Hormuz

The United States and Iran signed a preliminary memorandum of understanding aimed at ending the war, reopening the Strait of Hormuz to shipping, and launching further negotiations on nuclear and sanctions issues. The United States and Iran have signed a preliminary memorandum of understanding that commits both sides to ending the conflict and reopening the Strait of Hormuz, the critical waterway through which a significant share of the world's oil exports pass. The agreement was signed by both governments and made public by the Trump administration. Under the initial terms, Iran would receive sanctions relief in exchange for commitments on nuclear activity, with the most difficult and specific concessions — including the nuclear program's ultimate disposition — deferred to future negotiating rounds. The deal does not resolve the underlying conflict in full. Analysts and former diplomats have offered divergent assessments of the terms. Some view the agreement as granting Iran substantial economic benefits, particularly restored access to oil revenues, while requiring relatively modest immediate concessions. Others describe it as a workable framework for de-escalation. President Trump made public statements that military action could resume if Iran fails to uphold the agreement's terms. Oil prices fell following the announcement, as markets anticipated resumed shipping through the strait. The Strait of Hormuz carries an estimated 20 percent of global oil trade, and its effective closure had contributed to elevated energy prices in the United States and abroad. Further negotiations on harder issues — including the specifics of Iran's nuclear program and long-term sanctions architecture — are expected to follow. The agreement does not constitute a formal treaty and its durability depends on subsequent rounds of talks.

Supreme Court Rules Unanimously That Law Barring Marijuana Users from Owning Guns Violates Second Amendment

The Supreme Court ruled 9-0 that a federal law used to prosecute a Texas man for possessing a firearm while being a marijuana user violated the Second Amendment and is unconstitutionally vague, extending a line of rulings expanding gun rights. The Supreme Court ruled unanimously in favor of a Texas man who challenged a federal law that bars anyone who uses illegal drugs from possessing a firearm. The case centered on Ali Danial Hemani, who was charged with felony gun possession after acknowledging marijuana use. He was not accused of using the weapon while under the influence or of any other crime. The court held that the law, as applied to his circumstances, violated the Second Amendment and was unconstitutionally vague. The 9-0 decision continues a recent line of Supreme Court rulings that have significantly expanded gun rights protections under the Second Amendment. The ruling raises broader questions about how federal firearms laws interact with state-level marijuana legalization. Thirty-eight states have legalized marijuana in some form, creating a tension between state law — where marijuana use may be permitted — and federal law, which still classifies it as an illegal substance and uses that status as grounds to restrict firearm ownership. Legal observers expect the ruling to prompt challenges to similar firearms restrictions applied to other categories of drug users.

UK Study Finds HPV Vaccine Has Reduced Cervical Cancer Deaths in Young Women to Near Zero

A new study finds that young women in the UK who received the HPV vaccine beginning in 2008 now face close to zero risk of dying from cervical cancer, with hundreds of lives estimated to have been saved. A study examining the long-term outcomes of the UK's HPV vaccination program — which began offering the vaccine to school-age girls in 2008 — found that vaccinated women now have a risk of cervical cancer death that is close to zero. Researchers estimated that hundreds of lives have been saved as a result of the program. The findings represent one of the clearest demonstrations to date of the population-level impact of widespread HPV vaccination. Cervical cancer is caused in the vast majority of cases by persistent infection with certain strains of the human papillomavirus. The HPV vaccine, when administered before exposure to the virus, is highly effective at preventing the strains most associated with cancer. The results have implications for health policy discussions in the United States, where HPV vaccination rates among adolescents remain below recommended levels.
