New Study Finds Climate Feedback Loops Could Amplify Global Warming by Up to 30%

A new study found that rising temperatures are transforming forests, wetlands, and permafrost into net sources of greenhouse gas emissions, potentially amplifying overall global warming by as much as 30% beyond current projections. A peer-reviewed study has found that as global temperatures rise, natural ecosystems including permafrost, wetlands, and forests are increasingly releasing stored carbon dioxide and methane rather than absorbing them. These emissions from natural sources create what scientists call feedback loops — cycles where warming causes additional warming — that the study estimates could worsen overall global heating by as much as 30% beyond what would otherwise occur from fossil fuel emissions alone. Permafrost in high-latitude regions is of particular concern, as thawing ground releases methane, a potent greenhouse gas. The findings add to a body of evidence suggesting that current climate projections, which rely heavily on natural ecosystems continuing to absorb carbon, may underestimate the pace and severity of future warming. The study has implications for international climate policy and for targets set under existing international agreements.
