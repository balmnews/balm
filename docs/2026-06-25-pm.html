Supreme Court Rules 6-3 That Government May Turn Away Asylum Seekers at the Border

The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that federal law permits the government to prevent asylum seekers from physically entering the United States at the border, effectively allowing the administration to reinstate a policy of turning migrants away before they can file asylum claims. The Supreme Court issued a 6-3 ruling on Thursday allowing the Trump administration to reinstate its policy of turning back asylum seekers at the U.S.-Mexico border before they can set foot on American soil. The decision fundamentally alters how the U.S. asylum system operates at the border. Under the ruling, the government may use what officials call a 'metering' or 'turn-back' policy, preventing migrants from physically entering the country and thereby blocking them from filing asylum claims under existing law, which requires a person to be present on U.S. soil to seek asylum. The court's majority held that federal law permits the executive branch to restrict asylum access in this way. A dissenting justice noted that the ruling could create new incentives for unauthorized crossings at points away from official ports of entry. Immigration advocacy groups and some members of Congress criticized the decision sharply, with some characterizing it as a significant rollback of protections for people fleeing persecution. The ruling was one of two major immigration decisions issued by the court on Thursday. It was accompanied by a separate ruling allowing the administration to strip Temporary Protected Status from Haitian and Syrian nationals — a decision that could affect hundreds of thousands of people who have been living and working legally in the United States for years. At least one Republican House member broke with the administration on the TPS ruling, arguing that ending Haitian TPS in particular would create a strain on the U.S. healthcare system, as many TPS holders work in medical fields.

Supreme Court Allows Administration to End Protected Status for Haitian and Syrian Immigrants

The Supreme Court ruled that the president has unreviewable authority to terminate Temporary Protected Status, clearing the way for the deportation of hundreds of thousands of Haitian and Syrian nationals who have lived in the United States under the program. In a second major immigration ruling issued Thursday, the Supreme Court allowed the Trump administration to end Temporary Protected Status for Haitian and Syrian nationals, a decision that could affect hundreds of thousands of people currently living and working legally in the United States. Writing for the majority, Justice Samuel Alito concluded that under the TPS statute, the president holds unreviewable authority to terminate the program, meaning courts cannot intervene to block such a decision. TPS is a humanitarian designation that allows nationals from countries experiencing armed conflict, natural disasters, or other extraordinary conditions to live and work in the United States temporarily. The ruling opens a legal path for the administration to begin deportation proceedings against those whose status is terminated. The decision drew criticism from immigration groups and some lawmakers. At least one House Republican, Representative Mike Lawler, publicly opposed the ruling's implications, arguing that ending Haitian TPS poses a specific risk to the U.S. healthcare workforce, where many TPS holders are employed. The ruling, taken together with the court's separate asylum decision issued the same day, represents a substantial shift in the legal landscape governing immigration enforcement.

Supreme Court Rules for Monsanto, Blocking Thousands of Roundup Cancer Lawsuits

The Supreme Court ruled in favor of Monsanto's parent company, holding that federal pesticide label regulations preempt state-law claims that the company failed to warn users of cancer risks associated with its Roundup weedkiller. The decision is expected to block thousands of pending lawsuits. The Supreme Court ruled Thursday in Monsanto v. Durnell that federal law governing pesticide labeling takes precedence over state-level failure-to-warn claims, a decision that is expected to effectively end thousands of lawsuits filed by people who allege they developed cancer after using the herbicide Roundup. The central legal question was whether the federal pesticide regulatory framework — which sets standards for what must appear on product labels — overrides state tort claims that a manufacturer failed to adequately warn consumers of health risks. The court found that it does. The case was brought by Missouri resident John Durnell, who alleged the company did not sufficiently warn users about a potential link between the active ingredient glyphosate and certain cancers. The ruling does not settle the underlying scientific dispute about glyphosate's health effects, which has been contested among researchers and regulatory bodies. However, it removes a key legal avenue that plaintiffs have used in recent years to pursue compensation. The decision is significant for the broader question of when federal regulatory approval of a product's label shields a company from state-based liability, with implications that could extend to other industries beyond agriculture.

Supreme Court Strikes Down Hawaii Law Requiring Permit to Carry a Firearm in Public

The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that Hawaii's requirement to obtain prior government permission before carrying a firearm in public places an undue burden on Second Amendment rights, striking down the state restriction. The Supreme Court issued a 6-3 ruling Thursday striking down Hawaii's law requiring residents to obtain advance government approval before carrying a firearm in public. The court's majority held that requiring permission in advance — which critics referred to as a 'vampire rule' because it required individuals to seek authorization before exercising a constitutional right — constitutes an undue burden on the right to possess and carry a firearm under the Second Amendment. The decision fell along ideological lines, with the court's six conservative justices in the majority and three liberal justices dissenting. The ruling adds to a line of Second Amendment cases in which the court has expanded gun rights protections. Hawaii had one of the most restrictive carry permitting systems in the country. The decision will require the state to revise its framework and may have implications for other states with similar advance-permission structures.

Iran Strikes Cargo Ship in Strait of Hormuz, Halting UN-Backed Evacuation Effort

Iran struck a cargo ship near the coast of Oman in the Strait of Hormuz, prompting a United Nations agency to pause its evacuation plan for vessels trapped in the Persian Gulf. Iran also rejected a separate UN-backed proposal for a broader shipping framework. Oil prices fell to pre-conflict levels as some tankers had already departed. A cargo ship was struck by a projectile in the Strait of Hormuz near the coast of Oman on Thursday, according to U.S., Iranian, and British military officials. No casualties were reported aboard the vessel. The attack prompted a United Nations agency to pause its coordinated plan to evacuate ships from the Persian Gulf, an effort that had been gaining momentum as several tankers successfully transited the strait earlier in the day. Iran had also separately rejected a UN-backed proposal, supported by Oman, to consult on establishing a new framework for safe shipping passage through the strait. The rejection created additional uncertainty for commercial vessels still in the region. The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world's most critical shipping routes, carrying a substantial share of global oil and gas exports. The attack represents a setback for diplomatic efforts to restore stable commercial traffic through the waterway, and U.S. officials were seeking regional support for a broader peace framework.

Europe's Second Major Heat Wave This Season Sets Records, Strains Health Systems

A severe heat wave across Europe broke June temperature records in the UK and Switzerland, caused heat-linked deaths in France, Spain, and Italy, strained hospitals in England, and prompted France to shut down some nuclear reactors. Temperatures in parts of Germany are forecast to reach 40°C. A sustained and intense heat wave — Europe's second since May — has broken June temperature records across multiple countries and placed significant pressure on public health infrastructure. The United Kingdom recorded a provisional high of 36.7°C in Merryfield, Somerset, its hottest June day on record. Switzerland also reported its highest-ever June temperature. In France, authorities linked the deaths of at least three young children to the heat, and officials warned that even young, healthy people face health risks. A temporary water-use restriction was imposed in parts of southeast England. France's teaching unions called on staff to consider striking over what they described as dangerous conditions in overheated schools, which lack air conditioning. The heat wave is consistent with long-term warming trends scientists attribute to climate change. Temperatures were beginning to shift eastward toward Germany and Central Europe as of Thursday.

Ebola Outbreak Expands as Scientists Seek Origin and Evaluate Containment Prospects

An ongoing Ebola outbreak involving the Bundibugyo variant is expanding, with scientists still unable to identify the animal reservoir from which the virus originates. Contact tracing has improved, but treatments remain months away and the trajectory of the outbreak is uncertain. An Ebola outbreak caused by the Bundibugyo virus strain is growing, and scientists have not yet identified the animal species that serves as its persistent reservoir — a gap that complicates efforts to prevent future spillover events into human populations. Researchers believe the virus cycles through an as-yet-unknown animal host and occasionally transmits to humans, but decades of investigation have not produced a confirmed answer. On the containment side, contact tracing operations have improved since the outbreak began, which public health officials consider a positive indicator. However, effective treatments for the Bundibugyo strain are not expected to be available for several months, leaving response teams reliant on supportive care and isolation protocols. Five key factors are shaping whether the outbreak can be slowed: the pace of contact tracing, the availability of treatments, the geographic spread of cases, community trust in health authorities, and the capacity of local health systems. The outbreak is described as among the fastest-expanding on record for this strain. While the affected region is in Africa, Ebola outbreaks carry international public health significance given the potential for cross-border spread and the precedent set by prior outbreaks that required global response coordination.

FDA Approves Keytruda Combination as First-Line Treatment for Advanced Triple-Negative Breast Cancer

The FDA approved two forms of pembrolizumab (Keytruda and Keytruda Qlex), each combined with Trodelvy, as first-line treatments for patients with PD-L1-positive advanced triple-negative breast cancer, one of the most difficult-to-treat forms of the disease. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has approved two formulations of pembrolizumab — sold as Keytruda and Keytruda Qlex — each in combination with the antibody-drug conjugate sacituzumab govitecan (Trodelvy) for the first-line treatment of advanced triple-negative breast cancer in patients whose tumors express the PD-L1 biomarker at a combined positive score of 10 or higher. Triple-negative breast cancer is an aggressive subtype that does not respond to hormone therapies and has historically had limited treatment options. First-line approval means these combinations can now be used as an initial treatment rather than only after other therapies have failed. Keytruda Qlex is a newer subcutaneous formulation that combines pembrolizumab with an enzyme that allows injection under the skin rather than intravenous infusion. The approval adds a new standard-of-care option for a patient population with significant unmet medical need.

NASA Rover Detects Complex Carbon Molecules in Martian Rocks Flagged for Potential Signs of Ancient Life

NASA's Perseverance rover has identified complex organic carbon molecules in Martian mudstones at a site already under study for potential signatures of ancient microbial life, adding to the scientific interest in the rock samples. NASA's Perseverance rover has detected complex carbon-bearing molecules in mudstone samples collected from a Martian geological feature called the Bright Angel outcrop, using its onboard Sherloc instrument. The Bright Angel site was already of significant scientific interest because earlier analyses suggested it might bear chemical signatures consistent with ancient microbial life — though no definitive biological evidence has been established. The detection of organic carbon adds another data point to the scientific case for examining these rocks in greater detail. Organic molecules can form through geological as well as biological processes, so their presence does not confirm the existence of past life. The findings strengthen the scientific rationale for returning these samples to Earth for more comprehensive laboratory analysis, which remains the primary goal of NASA's Mars Sample Return program.

Twin Earthquakes Kill Hundreds in Venezuela, Thousands Feared Trapped

Two earthquakes measuring 7.2 and 7.5 in magnitude struck Venezuela's northern coast within one minute of each other, killing at least 188 people and injuring more than 1,500. Thousands more are feared dead as rescue teams search the rubble. Two powerful earthquakes struck Venezuela's northern coast in rapid succession on Wednesday evening, leaving at least 188 people confirmed dead and more than 1,500 injured. Authorities and residents fear the final death toll could reach into the thousands, with rescue teams working through collapsed buildings along the heavily damaged coastline. The port city of La Guaira was among the hardest hit, with residents reporting hundreds of people trapped in rubble. The capital Caracas and surrounding areas also sustained significant damage, including building collapses. The 7.2- and 7.5-magnitude quakes struck roughly one minute apart — a seismic pairing scientists call a 'doublet.' Researchers believe the two events may have involved separate but intersecting fault lines in a tectonically complex zone where the Caribbean plate moves eastward relative to the South American plate. Venezuela has not experienced earthquakes of this scale in more than a century. The U.S. Secretary of State said the Defense Department would assist in deploying search and rescue teams to the affected area. President Trump described the disaster as causing a 'devastating number of deaths' and directed U.S. agencies to prepare assistance. Venezuela's government declared a state of emergency. The earthquakes represent a compounding blow to a country already in a fragile political and economic state.
