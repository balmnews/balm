Gloria Steinem, Feminist Journalist and Activist, Dies at 92

Gloria Steinem, the journalist and activist who became a central figure in the American women's rights movement for more than five decades, died on September 2 at her home in New York City. She was 92. Gloria Steinem, one of the most prominent voices of the American women's rights movement, died on September 2 at her home in New York City at the age of 92. Her foundation announced she passed away peacefully, surrounded by family and friends. Tributes arrived quickly from political figures, artists, and journalists across the country. Steinem built her public career as a journalist before becoming a central organizer and spokesperson for the women's liberation movement beginning in the late 1960s. Over more than five decades of public life, she challenged prevailing ideas about gender roles and political power, helping shift both law and culture in the United States. She co-founded Ms. magazine in 1972, which became an influential platform for feminist journalism. Later in her life, Steinem described a sense of personal freedom that came with age, telling interviewers that she felt released from expectations imposed by gender. She remained active in advocacy work well into her eighties. Her death prompted widespread remembrance from elected officials, filmmakers, and activists who described her as both an inspiration and, for many, a personal mentor.

Federal Judge Blocks Trump's Second Executive Order on Birthright Citizenship

A federal judge has temporarily blocked President Trump's second executive order seeking to limit birthright citizenship, ruling it is almost certainly unconstitutional. The order targeted citizenship for children born in the US to parents without legal status. A federal judge has issued a temporary block on President Trump's second executive order aimed at restricting birthright citizenship — the constitutional principle, derived from the Fourteenth Amendment, that all individuals born on US soil are automatically American citizens. The judge, appointed during the Biden administration, ruled that the order is almost certainly unconstitutional based on the existing text of the Fourteenth Amendment and longstanding legal precedent. This is Trump's second attempt via executive action to restrict birthright citizenship. An earlier order was also blocked by courts. The administration has argued that the citizenship guarantee should not apply to children of individuals who are in the country without legal authorization or on temporary visas. That interpretation has been rejected by the majority of constitutional legal scholars and by multiple courts. The White House criticized the ruling, describing the judge as an activist. The case is likely to continue through the federal courts, and the administration may seek emergency relief from the Supreme Court. Birthright citizenship affects tens of thousands of births annually and has been a recurring focus of immigration policy debate. Any eventual Supreme Court ruling on the question would carry broad implications for immigration law and constitutional interpretation.

UN Report Acknowledges Earth Will Exceed 1.5-Degree Warming Limit, Outlines Path to Reduce Temperatures

A new United Nations report states that global temperatures will exceed the 1.5-degree Celsius threshold identified as a key climate danger zone, and outlines steps the world would need to take to eventually bring temperatures back below that level. The United Nations has released a report acknowledging that the world will exceed the 1.5-degree Celsius warming limit established by the Paris Agreement as a threshold for preventing the most severe climate harms. The report represents a significant shift in the international climate conversation, moving from the question of whether the limit can be avoided to how temperatures might eventually be brought back below it after being exceeded. Scientists refer to this as a temporary overshoot scenario. The 1.5-degree limit was set in 2015 as a target to minimize risks including sea level rise, extreme weather, ecosystem collapse, and threats to food and water security. Exceeding it does not mean those harms become inevitable at a fixed point, but the report acknowledges that some damage will be locked in. The UN outlined a range of measures that would be required to draw temperatures back down, including dramatic reductions in greenhouse gas emissions, expansion of carbon removal technologies, and changes to land use. The report comes as global temperatures have repeatedly set new records and as debate continues in many countries over the pace and cost of decarbonization. For the United States, the findings add context to ongoing domestic policy debates about climate regulation, energy production, and international climate commitments.

Genetically Modified Pig Kidney Functions in Human Patient for Record 271 Days Before Human Transplant

A 66-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease lived for a record 271 days with a transplanted genetically modified pig kidney before successfully receiving a human kidney, marking a milestone in xenotransplantation research. A 66-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease caused by type 2 diabetes received a genetically modified pig kidney and lived with the organ functioning in his body for a record 271 days — approximately nine months — before undergoing a successful human kidney transplant. The case is described as a world first in which a pig organ served as a functional bridge to a human transplant rather than as a permanent replacement. The patient, identified in reports as Tim Andrews, was seriously ill before the procedure and would not otherwise have survived long enough to receive a human organ. The pig kidney used in the transplant was genetically modified to make it more compatible with the human immune system, reducing the risk of rejection. Xenotransplantation — the transplanting of organs from one species to another — has been an active area of medical research for decades, driven by a persistent global shortage of donor organs. Previous attempts to transplant pig kidneys into humans had demonstrated shorter periods of function. The success of this case is expected to advance clinical trials and regulatory discussions around xenotransplantation. Researchers expressed optimism that the results could eventually increase the availability of organs for patients on long transplant waiting lists, though substantial further research is required before the approach could become standard clinical practice.
