Study Finds US Military Veterans Have Higher PFAS Levels in Blood Than Civilians

A new peer-reviewed study found that US military veterans have significantly higher levels of PFAS chemicals in their blood than non-veterans, linked to contamination at military bases from firefighting foam. A peer-reviewed study has found that many US military veterans carry substantially higher concentrations of PFAS chemicals — synthetic compounds often called forever chemicals because they do not break down in the environment or the human body — than their civilian counterparts. The elevated levels are linked to contamination of groundwater, surface water, and soil at and around military installations, where a type of firefighting foam containing PFAS compounds was used extensively for decades. PFAS exposure has been associated with a range of health concerns including certain cancers, thyroid disorders, and immune system effects, though research into long-term health outcomes is ongoing. The findings add to existing evidence of environmental health risks for service members and veterans who trained or were stationed at affected bases. The study raises further questions about the scope of veterans' health monitoring and the adequacy of cleanup efforts at contaminated military sites across the country.
