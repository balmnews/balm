Supreme Court Upholds Birthright Citizenship, Striking Down Trump Executive Order

The Supreme Court ruled that children born in the United States are citizens regardless of their parents' immigration status, striking down a Trump executive order that sought to end birthright citizenship. Trump subsequently indicated he would pursue the change through Congress. The Supreme Court ruled on Tuesday that birthright citizenship — the constitutional right of any person born on American soil to be a United States citizen — cannot be ended by executive order. The decision struck down an order President Trump signed on the first day of his second term, which sought to deny citizenship to children born in the US to parents who are in the country without legal status or on temporary visas. The court's opinion, written for the majority, held that the administration violated the Fourteenth Amendment, which guarantees citizenship to all persons born in the United States. The ruling was described by the court itself as breaking no new constitutional ground, affirming a legal principle that has been settled since the late nineteenth century. The court is currently composed of six conservative and three liberal justices. The decision was welcomed by civil rights organizations and immigrant advocacy groups. Trump responded by announcing that Republicans would pursue an end to birthright citizenship through legislation in Congress, calling for a national Republican convention before the midterm elections. The case had been brought by a coalition of states and by immigrant families directly affected by the executive order. Legal analysts noted that while the ruling provides immediate protection for families already in legal limbo, a legislative path to changing birthright citizenship would face significant constitutional hurdles, as the right is embedded in the Fourteenth Amendment itself.

Supreme Court Rules States May Ban Transgender Athletes from Female Sports Teams

The Supreme Court upheld laws in West Virginia and Idaho that prohibit transgender girls and women from competing on female sports teams in schools and colleges. The ruling allows other states to enforce similar laws. The Supreme Court ruled on Tuesday that states may legally prohibit transgender athletes from competing on girls' and women's sports teams in schools and colleges. The case centered on laws in West Virginia and Idaho that bar transgender girls and women from female athletic competition. The court's conservative majority upheld both laws, finding that states have the authority to make such distinctions in athletic settings. In a concurring opinion, Justice Clarence Thomas addressed the underlying question of sex and gender identity directly. The ruling was praised by some athletes and sports advocates as a protection of competitive fairness in women's sports, while civil rights and LGBTQ organizations described it as harmful to transgender youth. President Trump called it a positive outcome, and First Lady Melania Trump also issued a public statement in response. Transgender youth athletes and their advocates stated they would continue to participate in sports and to pursue legal and legislative avenues for equal access. The decision does not mandate that states adopt such bans, but removes the constitutional obstacle for states that have enacted them. The ruling is expected to affect ongoing legal challenges in other states with similar laws.
