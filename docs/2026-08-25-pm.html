The United Nations and the International Committee of the Red Cross jointly urged countries to adopt binding bans and restrictions on autonomous weapons systems, warning that risks from the technology have intensified. The United Nations and the International Committee of the Red Cross issued a joint call for countries to establish international legal rules governing autonomous weapons systems — military platforms capable of selecting and engaging targets without direct human control, sometimes called killer robots. The two organizations said the risks posed by these weapons have intensified as the technology has advanced and its use has expanded in active conflicts. They called for binding international agreements that would include both prohibitions and restrictions on specific categories of autonomous weapons use. Autonomous weapons have been deployed or tested in several recent conflicts, and no binding international treaty currently governs their use. Study Finds at Least One in Four Former NFL Players Who Died Had Degenerative Brain Disease

A new study found that at least one in four former NFL players who died between 2016 and 2021 had chronic traumatic encephalopathy, a degenerative brain disease. Researchers said the true prevalence may be substantially higher. A new study examining the brains of former NFL players who died between 2016 and 2021 found that at least one in four showed evidence of chronic traumatic encephalopathy, commonly known as CTE, a degenerative brain disease associated with repeated head impacts. Researchers noted that the actual prevalence is likely higher than the study's figures suggest, due to limitations in how brain tissue is collected and analyzed after death. CTE can only be diagnosed posthumously through brain tissue examination and is associated with symptoms including memory loss, depression, and behavioral changes during life. The study represents one of the more comprehensive assessments of CTE prevalence in a defined population of former professional football players and adds to a growing body of research linking the sport to long-term neurological harm. The findings have implications for ongoing discussions about player safety, equipment standards, and the long-term health responsibilities of professional sports leagues.
