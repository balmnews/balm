President Trump signed an executive order Monday seeking to significantly alter the federal childhood vaccination schedule. The order recommends vaccinating children against 11 diseases, down from the 18 currently recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. It also directs that the combined measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine — commonly known as the MMR shot — be separated into three individual vaccines administered during separate appointments. The order attempts to formalize and expand changes the administration had already sought to make to CDC vaccination recommendations earlier this year. Federal courts had previously blocked some of those earlier efforts. The order also directs the Department of Health and Human Services to conduct additional vaccine research. The CDC's vaccine schedule is set by agency scientists and advisory committees based on clinical data; executive orders do not automatically override that process, and legal challenges are expected. Health officials and pediatric medical organizations have long maintained that the current vaccine schedule is safe and evidence-based. Separating the MMR vaccine into individual doses would require additional medical visits and could reduce overall vaccination rates, according to public health experts. The order was developed in coordination with Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who has long questioned the existing vaccine schedule.

July Was the Hottest Month Ever Recorded in the US; Global Oceans Also Set July Temperature Records

NOAA confirmed July 2026 was the hottest month ever recorded in the contiguous United States, averaging 3.3°F above the 20th-century mean. Separately, global ocean surface temperatures also reached record highs for July, with European nations breaking all-time heat records. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration confirmed that July 2026 was the hottest month ever recorded in the contiguous United States across 132 years of reliable measurement. The average temperature was 3.3 degrees Fahrenheit — or 1.8 degrees Celsius — above the 20th-century baseline, surpassing all previous monthly records. Repeated, widespread heatwaves drove the reading. Scientists attribute the elevated heat in part to human-caused climate change. Separately, the European Union's Copernicus climate change service reported that global ocean surface temperatures reached record highs for any July on record, with particularly extreme readings along the Atlantic coast and in the western Mediterranean. A developing El Niño pattern contributed to the ocean warming alongside longer-term climate trends. In Europe, Austria reached 41.2°C, Slovakia hit 42.2°C, and Hungary recorded 41.8°C — surpassing a temperature mark set in 1905. Severe marine heat stress was also documented. The combination of record land temperatures in the US, record ocean temperatures globally, and extreme heat across Europe represents a convergence of climate indicators that scientists say reflects accelerating trends. Wildfires burning across western Europe were linked to the extreme heat and drought conditions. In the United States, the record July temperatures came against a backdrop of ongoing wildfires in the Pacific Northwest and severe drought in parts of the West.
