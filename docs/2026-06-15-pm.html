U.S. and Iran Sign Preliminary Agreement to End Conflict and Reopen Strait of Hormuz

The United States and Iran reached a preliminary deal to end their nearly four-month conflict and reopen the Strait of Hormuz to shipping, with a formal signing ceremony expected Friday in Switzerland. Major unresolved issues, including Iran's nuclear program, were deferred to further negotiations. The United States and Iran have agreed to a memorandum of understanding to end the conflict that began roughly fifteen weeks ago, with a formal signing ceremony scheduled for Friday in Switzerland, according to senior U.S. officials and key mediator Pakistan. President Trump announced the deal and ordered a halt to the U.S. naval blockade, declaring that shipping traffic in the Strait of Hormuz would gradually resume. The agreement represents the most significant development in the conflict that disrupted global energy markets and cost thousands of lives, the majority of them Iranian. The deal's core structure returns both sides broadly to prewar conditions. The Strait of Hormuz, which had been closed to tanker traffic since the conflict began, is expected to reopen under the terms of the agreement. Vice President Vance said the administration's key objectives — preventing Iran from obtaining a nuclear weapon, reopening the strait, and lowering energy prices — had been achieved. Iranian state media, however, characterized the agreement as a victory over the United States and Israel. Critical issues were explicitly deferred. The future of Iran's nuclear program will be subject to further negotiations, and Senator Lindsey Graham, a close ally of the president, cautioned that Washington and Tehran appear to have fundamentally different understandings of what was agreed. Israeli officials expressed uncertainty about the deal's implications for their security interests, and Israeli Defense Minister Katz said Israeli forces would remain in Lebanese security zones regardless. Markets responded sharply. Global oil prices fell five percent to below eighty-three dollars per barrel, a three-month low, and U.S. and European stock markets rose to record highs. Analysts cautioned, however, that a full recovery in energy supply could take months — even if the strait reopens on schedule — as shipping firms said they would need additional security guarantees before resuming transits. Lebanon, which was also affected by the broader regional conflict, saw some residents begin returning to southern villages shortly after the announcement, though Israeli armored vehicles remained in the area. The G7 summit, beginning in France, is expected to be significantly shaped by the deal, with Ukraine and Middle East stability on the agenda alongside it.

State PFAS Bans Reduce 'Forever Chemicals' in Clothing, Though Some Companies Still Exceed Limits

A new report finds that state laws banning PFAS chemicals in clothing and textiles have significantly reduced their presence in tested products, with about 80 percent of 115 items showing compliant levels. Some companies, however, still exceed the limits. State-level laws restricting the use of PFAS — a class of synthetic chemicals known as 'forever chemicals' because they do not break down in the environment or the human body — have produced measurable reductions in their presence in clothing and textiles, according to a new report. Testing of 115 products found that approximately 80 percent showed PFAS levels that comply with applicable regulations, a result that public health advocates describe as a significant improvement. However, the report also found that a portion of companies appear to have ignored the state bans, with some products still exceeding legal limits. PFAS chemicals have been widely used in clothing to provide water resistance and stain repellency, and have been linked to a range of health concerns including certain cancers, immune system effects, and hormone disruption. The findings underscore both the effectiveness of state-level regulatory action and the need for consistent enforcement to ensure compliance across the industry.

Study Finds Menopausal Hormone Therapy Associated with 69% Lower Risk of Low Bone Density

A retrospective study presented at the Endocrine Society's 2026 annual meeting found that women on menopausal hormone therapy had a 69 percent lower risk of low bone mineral density compared with women not using it. A retrospective study presented at ENDO 2026, the Endocrine Society's annual meeting in Chicago, found that women who used menopausal hormone therapy faced a significantly reduced risk of low bone mineral density — a risk reduction of approximately 69 percent compared with women who did not use the therapy. Low bone mineral density is a precursor to osteoporosis and increases the risk of fractures, which are a significant cause of disability and mortality in older women. As a retrospective study, the findings reflect associations observed in existing patient data rather than a controlled clinical trial, which means causation cannot be established from this research alone. Menopausal hormone therapy has been the subject of evolving medical guidance over recent decades, with earlier concerns about certain health risks having been refined by subsequent research that identified differences in risk profiles depending on the type, timing, and duration of therapy used.

United Kingdom Plans to Ban Social Media Access for Children Under 16 Starting in 2027

The U.K. government announced plans to prohibit children under 16 from using social media platforms including TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Snapchat, Facebook, and X, with the ban set to take effect in 2027. The policy makes Britain part of a growing group of countries implementing age-based social media restrictions. Prime Minister Keir Starmer announced that the United Kingdom will ban children under the age of 16 from accessing social media platforms beginning in 2027. The restriction would apply to major platforms including TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Snapchat, Facebook, and X. The government acknowledged that some teenagers may find ways to circumvent the ban but stated that children's safety and wellbeing justify the policy. The U.K. joins a growing number of countries moving toward age-based social media restrictions. Australia enacted a similar law in 2025, and several other nations have introduced comparable measures or are actively debating them. The policy places responsibility on platforms to verify user ages and enforce the restriction, though implementation details and enforcement mechanisms are still being developed. Experts and technology observers note that age verification at scale presents significant technical and privacy challenges, and that determined users can often bypass such restrictions through virtual private networks and other tools. Proponents argue that even an imperfect ban shifts the burden of proof from children and parents to the platforms themselves and establishes a clearer legal standard for child safety online. The announcement carries relevance for American policymakers and technology companies, as the U.K. decision could influence regulatory approaches in other markets, including the United States.

Colorado River Drought Deepens as States Face Growing Water Access Disputes

A prolonged drought is reducing water levels in the nation's largest reservoirs fed by the Colorado River, raising the prospect of litigation among the seven states that depend on it for water supplies. A sustained drought across the American West is driving down water levels in the major reservoirs fed by the Colorado River, including Lake Mead and Lake Powell, and sharpening conflicts among the seven U.S. states that rely on the river for water. The situation raises the possibility of legal action over water allocation rights as supplies fall short of long-standing agreements. The Colorado River is the primary water source for tens of millions of people across Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming, as well as for large areas of agriculture. The river's flows have been declining for decades due to a combination of overallocation — the river was apportioned based on historically high flow estimates — and the effects of a warming climate. Negotiations among the states over new water-sharing frameworks have been ongoing, but a lasting agreement that accounts for reduced water availability has not yet been reached, leaving the door open for legal disputes that could ultimately require federal or judicial resolution.
