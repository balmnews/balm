Africa's Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has warned that an ongoing Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has the potential to become the largest in recorded history. The warning centers on a critical gap in contact tracing: most people testing positive for the disease are not already known to health workers, indicating that transmission chains are spreading beyond the reach of current surveillance efforts. The specific Ebola variant involved is the Bundibugyo strain, which scientists believe persists in an as-yet-unidentified animal species and periodically crosses into human populations. Researchers have not been able to confirm which animal serves as the reservoir. The combination of lagging contact tracing and an unknown animal source complicates both containment and prevention efforts. While the outbreak is centered in Congo, Ebola outbreaks historically draw international health resources and monitoring given the potential for cross-border spread.

Cases of Powassan virus, a rare and potentially serious tick-borne illness, have been confirmed in multiple US states including Rhode Island and Maine. Health authorities say the disease is increasing in frequency. Health officials in multiple US states, including Rhode Island and Maine, have confirmed cases of Powassan virus, a rare tick-borne viral infection that can cause severe neurological illness. Public health authorities say the disease is becoming more common in the United States, though it remains uncommon overall. Powassan virus is transmitted through the bite of infected ticks and has no specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive. Unlike Lyme disease, Powassan can be transmitted very quickly after a tick attaches. Symptoms can include fever, headache, vomiting, and in serious cases, encephalitis or meningitis. The increase in confirmed cases coincides with peak tick season across the northeastern United States.
