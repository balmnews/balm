Houthi Forces Seize Red Sea Port of Mokha, Threatening Major Shipping Route

Houthi forces aligned with Iran have seized Mokha, a strategically significant port city on Yemen's Red Sea coast, routing forces aligned with Yemen's internationally recognized government. The capture marks one of the most significant military advances in the conflict in years and has immediate implications for global trade and energy markets. Mokha sits near the Bab al-Mandab Strait, the narrow waterway connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden. Roughly 10 percent of global seaborne trade passes through this corridor. Houthi control of Mokha would place the group in a substantially stronger position to interdict or threaten commercial shipping in the strait. The fighting has killed and wounded around 1,000 people and displaced tens of thousands, according to reports, making it the most intense violence in Yemen in years. Saudi Arabia and Houthi forces have exchanged airstrikes during the current round of hostilities. Financial markets responded quickly. Brent crude oil rose above $106 per barrel — a roughly 6 percent single-day increase — as traders assessed the risk to Red Sea shipping lanes. Government bond prices fell across major economies as rising oil prices heightened inflation concerns. The Houthis have previously disrupted Red Sea shipping, drawing US and allied military responses. The renewed fighting and territorial gain raise fresh questions about the security of one of the world's most important maritime corridors, with potential downstream effects on energy prices and the cost of goods for American consumers.

California Governor Gavin Newsom signed legislation prohibiting social media platforms from providing algorithmically driven addictive features to users under 16, the first law of its kind in the United States. California has enacted legislation that bars social media companies from offering addictive features — such as algorithmically curated recommendation feeds and auto-play functions — to users under the age of 16. Governor Gavin Newsom signed the bill into law, making California the first state to enact this type of restriction. The law does not ban minors from social media outright but targets specific platform design features that researchers and policymakers have linked to excessive use and potential harm to young people's mental health. Platforms will be required to restrict these features for underage users or disable them by default. The legislation is likely to face legal challenges from technology companies, who have argued that such requirements conflict with First Amendment protections and create significant technical and compliance challenges. Similar efforts at the federal level have stalled. Because California is the largest US state by population and home to most major technology companies, laws enacted there frequently set a de facto national standard. The state has previously led on privacy regulation and other technology policy areas in ways that have influenced practices well beyond its borders.

Study Finds Climate Feedback Loops From Forests and Permafrost Could Add 30% to Global Warming

A new study found that rising temperatures are causing forests, wetlands, and permafrost to release additional greenhouse gases, which could amplify global warming by up to 30% beyond current projections. A new scientific study concludes that natural systems — including forests, wetlands, and Arctic permafrost — are increasingly releasing carbon dioxide and methane as temperatures rise, creating feedback loops that could substantially worsen climate change. The study estimates these natural emissions could amplify global warming by as much as 30 percent beyond what would result from human-caused emissions alone. As permafrost in high-latitude regions thaws, it releases long-stored carbon. Warming forests and wetlands contribute additional methane and CO2 through decomposition and altered ecological processes. Feedback loops are among the more difficult variables to account for in climate modeling because they are self-reinforcing: warming causes natural systems to emit more greenhouse gases, which causes further warming. The 30 percent figure represents a high-end estimate under the study's modeling scenarios. The findings add urgency to debates about the pace of emissions reductions needed to meet international climate targets. If natural feedback emissions are as significant as the study suggests, the remaining carbon budget — the amount humans can emit while staying within temperature limits — is smaller than previously calculated.

Chemours, DuPont, and Corteva have agreed to a $455 million settlement with the state of North Carolina and 11 local government entities to resolve claims tied to contamination from per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, commonly known as PFAS or 'forever chemicals.' The contamination is linked to the Chemours Fayetteville Works plant and nearby sites. PFAS are a class of synthetic chemicals used in a wide range of industrial and consumer products. They are called 'forever chemicals' because they do not break down naturally in the environment or the human body. Exposure has been linked to a range of health conditions, including certain cancers, immune system effects, and developmental issues. The settlement resolves lawsuits that accused the companies of polluting drinking water supplies serving communities in the region. The $455 million will be distributed among the state and the 11 local entities, which will use the funds for remediation, water treatment, and related purposes. The settlement is among the larger PFAS-related legal resolutions involving these companies in recent years. DuPont, Chemours, and Corteva have faced PFAS litigation across multiple states. The agreement does not constitute an admission of liability by the companies.

Drug-Resistant Ringworm Fungus Has Spread to 29 Countries, Cases Tripling

A ringworm fungus resistant to standard antifungal treatments has spread to 29 countries, with reported cases tripling in recent years, according to a new study. A strain of ringworm — a common fungal skin infection — that resists standard antifungal medications has now been identified in 29 countries, with case numbers tripling in a relatively short period, according to research published this week. Ringworm is typically treated with over-the-counter or prescription antifungal creams and oral medications. The drug-resistant strain, known in clinical settings as Trichophyton indotineae, does not respond reliably to terbinafine, one of the most commonly prescribed treatments. Patients infected with this strain may require alternative medications, which are often more expensive, harder to access, or associated with more significant side effects. The fungus spreads through skin-to-skin contact and contact with contaminated surfaces or objects. The geographic spread to 29 countries — up from a much smaller number just a few years ago — reflects both increased global movement and potentially broader transmission within healthcare and community settings. Public health authorities recommend that patients who develop ringworm that does not improve with standard treatment consult a clinician, as laboratory testing can identify resistant strains and guide alternative treatment choices.
