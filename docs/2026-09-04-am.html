UN Acknowledges 1.5-Degree Warming Limit Will Be Exceeded, Outlines Path to Recovery

The United Nations has formally acknowledged that global temperatures will exceed the 1.5-degree Celsius threshold set by the Paris Agreement, shifting focus to strategies for eventually bringing temperatures back below that level. The United Nations released a report acknowledging that Earth will exceed the 1.5-degree Celsius warming limit established under the Paris Agreement — a threshold that scientists have long warned represents a boundary beyond which the most severe consequences of climate change become increasingly difficult to manage. The report represents a strategic shift in how the international community frames climate goals: rather than focusing solely on preventing the threshold from being crossed, it maps a path for returning temperatures to below the danger zone after the limit has been breached. The approach, sometimes called 'overshoot and recovery,' relies on continued reductions in greenhouse gas emissions combined with the eventual large-scale removal of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. The UN's acknowledgment that the 1.5-degree target will be missed is significant because it is the first time the body has stated this plainly as a baseline assumption rather than a risk to be avoided. The report does not abandon long-term climate targets but reframes the timeline and strategy for achieving them.

Genetically Modified Pig Kidney Functions in Human Patient for Record Nine Months Before Successful Transplant

A genetically modified pig kidney kept a 66-year-old man with end-stage kidney failure alive for nine months — a record duration — before he successfully received a human kidney transplant. A 66-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease, Tim Andrews, survived for nine months supported by a genetically modified kidney from a pig before receiving a human kidney transplant, setting a new record for the duration of functioning xenotransplantation — the transplantation of organs across species. The pig kidney, which came from an animal named Wilma, was implanted as a bridge to buy time while Andrews waited for a compatible human donor organ. Andrews's kidney failure was caused by type 2 diabetes. The procedure, reported Thursday, represents a significant milestone in the field of xenotransplantation, which has sought for decades to address the severe shortage of human donor organs by using genetically engineered animal organs as substitutes or temporary support. Earlier xenotransplantation attempts in humans had either failed more quickly or been performed in patients who did not survive long enough to receive a human organ. The successful bridge-to-transplant outcome opens new possibilities for how genetically modified animal organs might be used to extend patients' lives while they await conventional transplants.
