The Trump administration has proceeded with auto-enrolling more than 60 million American children in 530A savings accounts, a new type of federally established savings vehicle sometimes referred to as Trump Accounts. The enrollment has occurred without requiring parents to take any action. The accounts are structured as long-term investment vehicles, and families who do not wish to participate may opt out. The program represents one of the largest automatic enrollment initiatives in US financial history. Details about contribution rules, investment options, and tax treatment of the accounts are still being communicated to families. US Government Borrowing Costs Reach 24-Year High Amid Global Bond Market Decline

Yields on 10-year US Treasury bonds rose to their highest level in 24 years as a global bond selloff accelerated, driven by concerns over persistent oil-driven inflation and fears that US deficit levels may be unsustainable. UK 30-year bond yields simultaneously hit a 28-year high. A broad selloff in global government bond markets intensified on Thursday, pushing 10-year US Treasury yields to their highest point since 2002. The selloff reflects investor concern about two converging pressures: persistently high oil prices, which threaten to reignite inflation, and growing anxiety that US federal deficit levels are approaching unsustainable territory. In the UK, 30-year government bond yields reached a 28-year high, indicating that the stress is not limited to US markets. Rising bond yields translate directly into higher borrowing costs for governments and, through mechanisms such as mortgage rates, for households and businesses. For American consumers, the bond market move reinforces an environment of elevated borrowing costs. The Federal Reserve is closely watched in this context, as the combination of persistent inflation signals and market pressure complicates decisions about future interest rate policy.

Federal Reserve Finalizes Changes to Bank Stress Test Procedures to Improve Transparency

The Federal Reserve finalized revisions to its annual bank stress testing process, aimed at increasing public transparency and reducing year-to-year swings in the capital requirements banks must hold as a result of the tests. The Federal Reserve finalized a set of changes to its annual stress testing program for large banks, the process used to assess whether institutions have sufficient capital to withstand severe economic downturns. The revisions are designed to make the methodology more transparent to the public and to financial institutions, and to reduce the volatility that has characterized year-to-year changes in stress test-driven capital requirements. Large swings in required capital buffers have been a concern for banks seeking to plan dividends and share buybacks. The Fed said the changes will improve the program's public accountability without undermining its effectiveness as a supervisory tool. The stress test program was established following the 2008 financial crisis as a safeguard against systemic banking failures.
