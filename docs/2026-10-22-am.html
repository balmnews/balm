Shallow coral reefs support an estimated quarter of all marine species and provide food, coastal protection, and economic livelihoods to hundreds of millions of people globally. Their collapse would represent one of the most significant ecological losses in recorded history, with cascading consequences for fisheries, coastal communities, and marine biodiversity. The report's authors described the findings as a warning that should prompt urgent action on greenhouse gas emissions. Ocean temperatures have risen significantly in recent years, with coral bleaching events — triggered when water becomes too warm for corals to survive — increasing in frequency and severity. Scientists noted that even modest reductions in warming trajectories could meaningfully improve outcomes for a portion of these ecosystems, making emissions reductions directly consequential for reef survival. The assessment does not represent a new scientific consensus but synthesizes existing research and is intended to inform international climate and biodiversity policy discussions.
