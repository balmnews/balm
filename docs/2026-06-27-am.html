TPS is a designation that allows people from countries experiencing disasters, conflict, or other extraordinary conditions to live and work legally in the U.S. on a temporary basis. The ruling clears the path for deportations on a large scale — estimates suggest the decision could affect more than one million people when accounting for both nationalities and their family members. The decision has also prompted concerns from demographers, who note that the U.S. population is already aging and trending toward decline, and that large-scale deportations could accelerate that trajectory. The ruling represents one of the most significant immigration decisions of the current Supreme Court term.

Supreme Court Rules for Roundup Maker, Blocking Thousands of Cancer Warning Lawsuits

The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in favor of the maker of Roundup herbicide, finding that federal pesticide labeling law preempts state-law lawsuits alleging the company failed to warn users of cancer risks. The decision is expected to block thousands of pending cases. The Supreme Court ruled in favor of the former Monsanto company, now owned by Bayer, in a case that is expected to eliminate thousands of lawsuits filed by individuals who allege they developed cancer from exposure to Roundup, a widely used herbicide whose active ingredient is glyphosate. The case, Monsanto v. Durnell, centered on whether federal pesticide law — which governs what warnings must appear on product labels — preempts state-law claims that the company inadequately warned consumers of health risks. The court ruled 6-3 that it does, meaning plaintiffs cannot use state courts to argue that the federally approved label was insufficient. The ruling has broad significance because it applies to a large body of pending litigation involving Roundup and could influence similar preemption arguments in other product liability areas. Bayer had faced billions of dollars in potential liability from the accumulated lawsuits. Dissenting justices argued the ruling leaves consumers without a legal remedy for harms they allege resulted from inadequate warnings.

Africa's CDC has warned that the ongoing Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo may become the largest ever recorded, with contact tracing lagging dangerously behind the spread. Most new cases are appearing among people not previously identified by health workers. Health authorities are raising concern that the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo could surpass all previous outbreaks in scale, according to Africa's Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The most urgent warning sign is that most people testing positive for Ebola are not already in the contact-tracing system maintained by health workers, suggesting the outbreak is spreading faster than it is being tracked. The current outbreak involves the Bundibugyo strain of the virus, which scientists believe persists in an animal species that occasionally transmits it to humans — though the specific animal reservoir has not yet been identified. Frontline Congolese workers conducting safe burials — a critical measure for preventing transmission — face both the risk of infection and hostility from communities frightened by the disease, complicating containment efforts. While the outbreak is centered in Africa, large Ebola outbreaks have historically drawn international attention and resources given their potential to spread, and prior outbreaks have prompted global health emergency responses.
