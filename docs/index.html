UN Climate Summit Ends Without Agreement as Fossil Fuel Language and Finance Demands Stall Talks

The UN's COP31 climate summit in Turkey concluded without a final agreement after negotiations over the proposed Antalya Pact collapsed. Dozens of nations objected to the draft text's failure to mention fossil fuels or increase financial assistance to developing countries. The 31st United Nations climate summit, held in Antalya, Turkey, ended without a consensus agreement after the proposed Antalya Pact — intended as the summit's central outcome document — failed to gain sufficient support in the final days of negotiations. Dozens of countries objected to the draft text because it did not include language referencing fossil fuels as a driver of climate change, and because it did not increase financial commitments to lower-income nations already experiencing significant climate impacts. The breakdown continues a pattern of difficult and sometimes inconclusive outcomes at annual UN climate gatherings. Analysts and climate policy experts caution that the failure of formal diplomatic talks does not mean all progress is stalled. Smaller multilateral agreements, commitments by subnational governments such as cities and states, and the continued rapid expansion of renewable energy capacity are cited as areas where momentum continues outside of the formal UN process. Some experts said the collapse of the summit could worsen economic inequality between wealthier and poorer nations, as developing countries had been counting on new financing commitments to fund climate adaptation and clean energy transitions. Nature writer Robert Macfarlane, delivering the BBC Reith Lectures, said in remarks recorded before the summit concluded that trust in the COP process to deliver consequential change is misplaced, and that he finds more hope in emerging coalitions outside the formal diplomatic framework. In Australia, climate campaigners are calling on the federal government to reject a proposed expansion of a Hunter Valley coal mine in the wake of the summit's failure, arguing the moment demands a clear signal against new fossil fuel development. The failed summit increases pressure on individual governments to take unilateral or bilateral action on emissions ahead of the next scheduled round of talks.

Measles Risk Growing Among US Adults, Including Some Who Were Previously Vaccinated

Medical experts say measles poses an increasing risk to adults in the United States, particularly those who are unvaccinated and those born before 1989 who may have received an older, less effective vaccine formulation. Medical experts are raising concern about growing measles risk among adults in the United States, not only among those who have never been vaccinated but also among a subset of vaccinated individuals. People born before 1989 may have received an earlier version of the measles vaccine that confers less durable protection than the two-dose MMR regimen that became standard after that year. As measles case counts in the US have risen in recent years — driven in part by declining vaccination rates in some communities — the pool of potentially susceptible adults is larger than previously assumed. Experts recommend that adults who are uncertain about their vaccination history or who received only one dose consult with a physician about whether a booster or additional dose is appropriate. Measles is highly contagious and can cause serious complications including pneumonia and encephalitis.

Doctors Report Increase in Parents Refusing Vitamin K Shots for Newborns

Physicians are reporting a rise in parents declining routine vitamin K injections for newborns, citing health misinformation. Without the shot, infants face a small but serious risk of a potentially fatal bleeding disorder. Neonatologists and pediatricians say they are encountering an increasing number of parents who refuse the standard vitamin K injection given to newborns shortly after birth, often citing misinformation circulating online that falsely links the shot to cancer or other harms. The injection is a routine preventive measure against vitamin K deficiency bleeding, a rare but potentially fatal condition in which an infant's blood does not clot properly. Newborns have naturally low vitamin K levels and cannot synthesize enough on their own in the first weeks of life. Without the injection, affected infants can suffer internal bleeding, including in the brain. Medical authorities including the American Academy of Pediatrics consider the injection safe and essential. Doctors describe the challenge of countering vaccine-adjacent misinformation in a brief window of time during or just after delivery.

Labor Department Proposes Rule That Could Limit ESG Investment Options in 401(k) Plans

The Labor Department has proposed a new rule that could discourage workplace retirement plans from offering investment options based on environmental, social, and governance criteria, revisiting a regulatory debate that has shifted with each recent administration. The Labor Department has proposed a regulation that may make it harder for employers to include ESG — environmental, social, and governance — investment options in 401(k) and similar workplace retirement plans. The proposal marks another reversal in an ongoing regulatory back-and-forth: the Biden administration had moved to make it easier for retirement plans to offer ESG funds, while the current administration is now moving in the opposite direction. Critics of ESG investments in retirement plans argue that fiduciaries should focus exclusively on financial returns for plan participants. Supporters argue that ESG factors can be financially material and that restricting such options limits employee choice. The rule is in a proposal stage and subject to a public comment period before it could take effect. For the tens of millions of Americans who hold 401(k) accounts, the practical impact would depend on whether employers respond by removing ESG options from their fund lineups.
