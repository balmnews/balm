Supreme Court Rules Unanimously That SEC Can Recover Illegal Profits Without Proving Victim Losses

The Supreme Court ruled unanimously that the Securities and Exchange Commission can recover illegally obtained financial gains from wrongdoers even when the agency cannot demonstrate that victims suffered a specific financial loss. The Supreme Court issued a unanimous ruling in favor of the Securities and Exchange Commission, holding that the agency has the authority to seek disgorgement — the return of illegally obtained profits — even in cases where the SEC cannot prove that identifiable victims suffered a quantifiable financial loss. Disgorgement is one of the primary enforcement tools the SEC uses to deter financial fraud and strip wrongdoers of the gains from illegal activity. The case had the potential to significantly limit the agency's enforcement reach if the court had ruled the other way. The unanimous nature of the decision, crossing ideological lines on the court, gives it particular legal weight. The ruling preserves a significant deterrence mechanism for securities regulators at a time when the SEC's enforcement posture has been a subject of political attention.

An Ebola outbreak caused by the Bundibugyo strain is spreading in the Democratic Republic of Congo. An Ebola outbreak driven by the Bundibugyo strain of the virus is expanding in the Democratic Republic of Congo, raising international concern. The Bundibugyo strain is a less common variety that has caused only two small outbreaks previously, and unlike the more familiar Zaire strain, there is currently no approved vaccine that works against it. Multiple vaccine candidates are in trials, but none has completed the process required for deployment. A significant barrier to controlling the outbreak is a severe shortage of diagnostic tests in Congo, leaving health workers without the tools to quickly identify and isolate cases, which allows the virus to spread unchecked. The Trump administration has not publicly stated whether Americans who may have been exposed will be permitted to return to the US for monitoring and treatment, departing from established procedures used in prior outbreaks. Scientists and public health officials have described the testing gap as a chronic problem rooted in long-term underinvestment in diagnostics for diseases that primarily affect lower-income countries. The outbreak's trajectory will depend heavily on the speed of vaccine trial completion, the availability of testing, and international coordination of the response.
