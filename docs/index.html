Israel Holds First Election Since October 7 Attack, with Record Turnout Reported

Israelis voted Tuesday in the first national election since the October 7, 2023 Hamas attacks, with record turnout reported. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu faces his closest rival, former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, in a race with no bloc holding a clear majority. Israelis cast ballots Tuesday in the country's first parliamentary election since the October 7, 2023 Hamas-led attacks, with early turnout figures described as the highest on record. The election is widely understood as a referendum on Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, Israel's longest-serving head of government, and on his handling of the security failures that preceded the attacks and the wars that followed. Polling ahead of the vote showed no bloc — neither Netanyahu's right-wing coalition nor the opposition — with a clear path to a parliamentary majority, meaning the next government will likely require weeks or months of coalition negotiations before a prime minister can be confirmed. Under Israel's proportional representation system, a party leader must assemble a coalition of smaller parties to reach a governing threshold. Netanyahu's main challenger is Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff. Former prime ministers Naftali Bennett and Avigdor Liberman, both of whom broke with Netanyahu, are also running and could function as swing factors in coalition negotiations. The campaign was fought along several axes: accountability for the October 7 failures, the ongoing wars in Gaza and elsewhere, the threat posed by Iran, and a broader debate about Israel's national identity — specifically, the tension between an increasingly assertive nationalist right and those who favor a more pluralist vision of the state. The outcome carries significant implications for U.S. foreign policy. Israel is a close American ally, and the direction of its next government will affect ceasefire negotiations, American military support, and the broader trajectory of the Middle East.

The vaccine works by teaching the immune system to recognize and attack cancer cells specific to an individual patient. Unlike traditional vaccines that prevent infections, cancer vaccines of this type are therapeutic — designed to treat an existing disease rather than prevent it. The melanoma results were described as a meaningful advance. Scientists cautioned that earlier-stage studies for other cancers are still ongoing and that broader applicability has not yet been established. The figure surpasses previous record estimates from prior summers, including the severe 2022 and 2003 heat events. The research uses excess mortality data — comparing actual death rates against expected rates for the same period — to attribute deaths to heat. This methodology is standard in epidemiology but produces estimates rather than exact figures. Extreme heat has emerged as one of the leading causes of weather-related mortality globally. Europe, with a large elderly population and building stock not historically designed for extreme heat, has been particularly affected. Scientists link the increasing frequency and severity of heat events to long-term warming trends. While centered in Europe, the findings are relevant to American readers both as a signal of global heat trends affecting the U.S. as well and as a data point informing ongoing policy debates about climate adaptation and public health infrastructure.
