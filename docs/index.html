COP31 Climate Summit Ends Without Agreement, Prompting Calls to Reform UN Process

The COP31 climate summit in Antalya concluded without a major agreement after the longest conference in the summit's history. Experts and former officials including Al Gore are calling for structural reforms to how UN climate decisions are made. The COP31 United Nations climate summit, co-chaired by Turkey and Australia and held in Antalya, ended without reaching its central goal: a sweeping agreement on emissions reduction and climate finance. The talks ran two days longer than scheduled, making them the longest in the summit's history, before collapsing without consensus. Analysts and diplomats cited several factors in the breakdown: the interests of oil-producing countries, a U.S. boycott of key sessions, and an unresolved dispute over how wealthy nations should finance climate adaptation in lower-income countries. The failure has prompted calls from international climate diplomacy experts, scientists, and former officials — including former U.S. Vice President Al Gore — for urgent reform of the UN's consensus-based decision-making rule, which currently allows any single country to block an agreement. Supporters of reform argue that requiring unanimous consent makes meaningful agreements structurally difficult. With the large multilateral framework stalled, attention is shifting to smaller bilateral and regional agreements, local government initiatives, and the continued expansion of clean energy deployment as alternative paths forward. The United States had already signaled reduced engagement with the UN climate process, further complicating efforts to forge a binding global pact. For American readers, the collapse affects U.S. trade partners, climate-linked supply chains, and the country's diplomatic standing in international environmental negotiations.

Measles Risk Rising Among U.S. Adults, Including Some Who Were Previously Vaccinated

Medical experts say measles poses a growing risk to American adults. The danger is highest among the unvaccinated, but vaccinated people born before 1989 may no longer have sufficient protection. Medical experts are raising concern about increasing measles risk among adults in the United States. The disease, once considered eliminated domestically, has seen a resurgence tied in part to declining vaccination rates in some communities. The risk is highest for unvaccinated individuals, but experts note that people who were vaccinated before 1989 may have received an earlier, less effective formulation of the vaccine and may no longer have sufficient immunity. Adults born before 1957 are generally considered immune due to likely prior exposure. Those born between 1957 and 1989 who received only one dose of the vaccine — which was the standard recommendation at the time — may be more vulnerable than they realize. The current recommendation is two doses of the measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine. Adults who are uncertain about their vaccination history or who received only one dose may wish to consult a physician about whether a booster is appropriate. Measles is highly contagious and can cause serious complications including pneumonia, encephalitis, and death.

A patient has completed one year living with a transplanted pig kidney, marking a milestone in the field of xenotransplantation — the use of animal organs in human patients. The kidney was genetically modified to reduce the likelihood of rejection by the human immune system. Medical experts said the one-year mark is significant because it demonstrates that a modified pig organ can function in a human body over a sustained period, not merely in the short term. Researchers hope that if genetically modified pigs can reliably supply functioning organs, they could substantially reduce that shortage. The patient's case adds to a small but growing body of evidence from recent xenotransplantation procedures.
