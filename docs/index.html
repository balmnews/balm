Alberta Voters Reject Independence Referendum by Wide Margin

Voters in Alberta, Canada's oil-rich western province, rejected a non-binding referendum on pursuing independence from Canada by approximately 76% to 24%, delivering a significant win for Prime Minister Mark Carney's push for national unity. With more than 1.5 million votes counted, roughly 76 percent of Alberta voters chose to remain in Canada, while about 24 percent supported beginning a process toward independence. The result was a decisive defeat for the separatist movement, which had gained momentum in recent years amid tensions over federal energy policy, taxation, and Alberta's sense of economic alienation within Confederation. The referendum was non-binding, meaning the outcome does not legally compel any action, but its margin carries significant political weight. Leaders of the separatist movement vowed to continue their efforts, though analysts note the scale of the defeat will make that difficult. The outcome is a boost for Prime Minister Mark Carney, who has made national unity a central theme of his government. Alberta, Canada's largest oil-producing province, has roughly three million eligible voters. The vote comes at a time when Canada-US relations are also under strain, adding complexity to the national unity debate.

Trump Administration Asks Supreme Court to Limit Lower Courts' Power to Block Federal Actions

The Trump administration has asked the Supreme Court to restrict the ability of lower federal courts to issue nationwide injunctions blocking administration policies, a move that would significantly affect how legal challenges to executive action function. The Trump administration has filed a request with the Supreme Court to limit the power of lower federal courts to issue nationwide injunctions — court orders that block administration policies from taking effect across the entire country while legal challenges proceed. Nationwide injunctions have been one of the primary legal tools used by opponents of Trump administration policies, and have repeatedly paused executive actions on immigration, trade, and other matters. The administration argues that such broad injunctions exceed the proper authority of district and appellate courts. The Supreme Court has in recent years signaled interest in revisiting the scope of such orders, and a ruling in favor of the administration's position could make it significantly harder to halt federal policies through litigation before a case reaches the Supreme Court. The request reflects a broader pattern in which the Trump administration has sought to expand executive authority and narrow the role of lower courts in checking it.
