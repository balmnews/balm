Dolores Huerta, who co-founded the United Farm Workers union with César Chávez and coined the rallying phrase "Sí, se puede," died Wednesday in California at age 96. Dolores Huerta, a labor organizer and civil rights activist who helped reshape conditions for agricultural workers in the United States, died Wednesday in California. She was 96. Huerta co-founded the United Farm Workers union alongside César Chávez in the 1960s, building a movement that advocated for better wages, safer working conditions, and basic labor rights for the farmworkers who harvested the nation's fruit and vegetable crops. The UFW became one of the most prominent labor organizations of the twentieth century, using strikes, boycotts, and nonviolent protest to achieve legislative and contractual gains. Huerta also coined the phrase "Sí, se puede" — meaning "Yes, it is possible" — which became a widespread rallying cry extending well beyond the labor movement. She began her career as a schoolteacher before turning to organizing full-time. Huerta continued as an active public voice on labor, feminist, and civil rights issues well into her later years.

New Study Estimates Three in Four Retired NFL Players Had CTE; Researchers Explore Potential Treatments

A new statistical study estimates that approximately three in four retired NFL players who have died likely had chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE. Separately, researchers are investigating new approaches to treatment, including near-infrared light therapy. A newly published study using statistical modeling has produced an estimate that approximately 75 percent of retired professional football players had chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, at the time of their deaths. CTE is a degenerative brain disease associated with repeated head impacts and has been documented in the brains of hundreds of former players through post-mortem examination. Because CTE can currently only be diagnosed after death, researchers have used statistical methods to extrapolate from known cases to the broader population of retired players — an approach the study's authors acknowledge carries uncertainty but provides a scale of the problem not previously quantified. Alongside the prevalence estimate, a separate line of research is exploring ways to treat or potentially prevent CTE's progression. One approach drawing attention is near-infrared light therapy, which researchers are studying for its possible effects on brain tissue inflammation and cellular repair. No treatment has yet been approved or demonstrated effective in clinical trials. The findings add new pressure on the NFL and youth football organizations to address player safety practices and concussion protocols.
