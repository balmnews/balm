FDA Approves New Drug for Pancreatic Cancer That Doubled Survival Time in Trial

The FDA approved daraxonrasib, a pill for pancreatic cancer that in a key clinical trial allowed patients to live twice as long as those who received chemotherapy. The US Food and Drug Administration has approved daraxonrasib, a new oral treatment for pancreatic cancer. In a pivotal clinical trial, patients who received the drug lived approximately twice as long as those treated with standard chemotherapy, a result researchers and oncologists have described as a significant advance for one of the most difficult-to-treat cancers. Pancreatic cancer has historically had among the lowest survival rates of any major cancer, in part because it is often detected late and responds poorly to existing treatments. The approval of daraxonrasib represents the first major treatment breakthrough for the disease in years. The drug works through a distinct mechanism from chemotherapy, targeting specific molecular pathways associated with pancreatic tumor growth. Experts quoted in coverage called the drug a potential game-changer for patients with limited prior options. The approval applies to a specific patient population defined in the trial; broader applicability will depend on further research.

A study of 70,000 US adults found that recipients of the Shingrix shingles vaccine had a 12% lower risk of heart attack and stroke compared to those who were not vaccinated. A study based on health records from 70,000 people in the United States found that individuals who received Shingrix, the currently recommended shingles vaccine, had a 12% lower risk of experiencing a heart attack or stroke compared to those who did not receive the vaccine. Researchers said the finding suggests the vaccine may provide cardiovascular benefits beyond its primary purpose of preventing shingles, a painful viral condition caused by the reactivation of the chickenpox virus. Shingrix is a two-dose vaccine recommended by public health authorities for adults 50 and older. Meta Settles with 47 States Over Child Safety Claims, Agrees to Platform Changes

Meta reached a settlement of up to $17 billion with 47 states, the District of Columbia, and US territories over claims its platforms harmed children, and agreed to add default safety restrictions for teenage users on Instagram and Facebook. Meta has agreed to pay up to $17 billion — figures vary slightly across sources, ranging from $16.7 billion to $18 billion — to settle claims brought by 47 states, the District of Columbia, and US territories alleging that its social media platforms deliberately endangered children. The settlement was reached during an ongoing federal trial in California and represents one of the largest consumer protection settlements in American history. Beyond the financial penalty, Meta agreed to make substantial changes to how teenagers use Instagram and Facebook. Key changes include making safety and time-limit settings the default for minor users, rather than requiring young people or parents to opt in. Previously, restrictive settings had to be actively selected. The agreement also calls on Meta to encourage other social media platforms to adopt similar protections. The settlement's scope is significant: it applies to users under 18 and addresses longstanding accusations that the platforms were engineered to maximize engagement in ways that harmed young people's mental health. State attorneys general had argued Meta knowingly exploited psychological vulnerabilities in minors. Not everyone views the outcome as sufficient. At least one key witness from the trial said publicly that the agreed limitations do not go far enough to stop ongoing harms to young users, comparing the new guardrails to superficial fixes rather than structural reform. Financially, analysts note that while the dollar figure is large by any standard, it is manageable for a company of Meta's scale. The settlement removes a significant legal cloud that had hung over the company, though it may set a precedent that prompts further regulatory action against other social media platforms. The exact final payment amount will depend on how funds are distributed among participating states and territories.

A flash flood on the Nepal-Tibet border killed more than 150 people and left hundreds missing, including 33 British citizens. A sudden and powerful flash flood swept through communities along the Bhote Koshi River on the border between Nepal and the Tibet region of China, killing more than 150 people and leaving several hundred more missing. The flood occurred at approximately 8:30 in the morning local time on the Tibetan side of the border before moving downstream through Nepal. Among those missing are more than 400 foreign travelers, including 33 British citizens — one of them a 13-year-old girl — according to Nepal's tourism board. The disaster struck with very little warning. Hydrologists noted that rainfall levels prior to the event were not high, which they said would have defeated standard flood warning systems, leaving communities with almost no time to respond before the wall of water and mud arrived. A 4.4-magnitude earthquake in the area is reported to have contributed to the flooding, with sources noting mudslides engulfing buildings in the Tibetan town of Gyirong. Search and rescue operations were underway, with families of missing travelers gathering at hospitals in Kathmandu awaiting news. The event highlights the vulnerability of Himalayan river valleys to sudden catastrophic flooding, a risk that scientists say is increasing as glacial and climate conditions in the region change. The presence of foreign tourists among the missing gives the disaster direct relevance to American and international readers whose nationals remain unaccounted for.
