Global Temperatures Exceed 2°C Above Preindustrial Levels for Six Consecutive Days for the First Time

The world has experienced six consecutive days of average temperatures 2°C or more above preindustrial levels for the first time on record, reaching a peak daily reading of 2.24°C above the baseline on Monday, with the streak forecast to continue. For the first time on record, the world has experienced six consecutive days of daily average temperatures at or above 2°C warmer than preindustrial levels, with the streak ongoing as of Wednesday. The peak daily reading reached 2.24°C above baseline on Monday. Scientists note that this multi-day streak does not in itself mean the long-term Paris Agreement target of limiting warming to 1.5°C or 2°C has been permanently breached — that determination is based on sustained averages over decades, not individual days or short periods. However, the streak is considered scientifically significant and unprecedented in the observational record. The 2°C threshold is the upper limit set by the 2015 Paris Agreement, beyond which scientists warn of substantially increased risks including more severe weather events, sea level rise, and ecosystem disruption. The record comes amid a broader context of a super El Niño weather pattern and continued accumulation of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. Climate scientists are tracking whether the streak will extend further and what it may indicate about the trajectory of near-term global temperatures.

New Close-Up Images of Asteroid Dimorphos Reveal Stadium-Sized Crater from NASA's 2022 Impact Test

New detailed images of asteroid Dimorphos show a stadium-sized crater and dramatically transformed surface resulting from NASA's 2022 deliberate spacecraft impact, providing the clearest assessment yet of the DART mission's effects on planetary defense. Four years after NASA deliberately crashed a spacecraft into the asteroid Dimorphos as a planetary defense test, new close-up images have revealed the full extent of the impact's effects on the asteroid's surface. The images show a crater estimated to be stadium-sized, along with significant changes to the asteroid's overall shape and composition. The 2022 Double Asteroid Redirection Test, known as DART, was the first demonstration of humanity's ability to intentionally alter the trajectory of a space object. Data from the mission confirmed that the impact successfully changed Dimorphos's orbital period around its larger companion asteroid, Didymos. The new imagery provides scientists with their most detailed view yet of the aftermath, allowing researchers to better understand how much momentum was transferred and what the structural consequences of such an impact are. These findings are directly relevant to future planetary defense planning — the ability to deflect an asteroid that might be on a collision course with Earth. Scientists say the results from DART and its subsequent analysis have significantly improved models for how a real deflection mission might be designed and executed.

The US Army carried out a controlled detonation of approximately 9,700 pounds of lead azide — the nation's entire stockpile of the bomb-making material — at an Iowa ammunition plant on Wednesday, citing risk of spontaneous explosion. Officials called it a success; the CDC activated an emergency response. The US Army on Wednesday morning detonated its entire national stockpile of lead azide, a bomb-making primary explosive, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middleton, Iowa. The controlled blast involved approximately 9,700 pounds — roughly five tons — of the material, which had been degrading over decades and was at risk of spontaneous, uncontrolled detonation as temperatures cooled. Army and plant officials described the operation as a 'huge success,' reporting that early lead readings in the surrounding area were promising. A visible cloud of smoke was seen approximately one mile from the detonation site. Prior to the blast, residents within a five-mile radius were evacuated, and FEMA advised residents within 20 miles to shelter in place until an all-clear was issued. Lead azide is a highly sensitive primary explosive used to initiate larger detonations; when it degrades, it can form crystals that are prone to spontaneous ignition. The Army said the controlled detonation was safer than allowing the material to continue degrading. The operation drew significant public concern in the days leading up to it, with residents and health professionals raising concerns about the potential spread of toxic lead dust over a wide area — reports cited a possible affected radius of up to 100 miles. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention activated an emergency response to support the Iowa Department of Public Health in monitoring for potential health effects. Doctors noted that lead exposure poses serious health risks, particularly for children. As of the detonation, officials said no elevated lead readings had been detected in initial monitoring.
