International Court of Justice Rules Myanmar Committed Genocide Against Rohingya

The International Court of Justice ruled that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya Muslim minority during a 2017 military operation, marking the first time the court has found a state culpable of violating the 1948 Genocide Convention. The court ordered Myanmar to allow displaced persons to return home. The United Nations' principal judicial body, the International Court of Justice, issued a landmark ruling finding Myanmar responsible for genocide against the Rohingya minority. The judgment centered on military operations conducted in northern Rakhine State in 2017, during which thousands of Rohingya people were killed and hundreds of thousands were driven into neighboring Bangladesh and other countries. The court found that Myanmar's security forces acted with genocidal intent during those operations. This marks the first time in the court's history that a state has been found culpable of violating the 1948 Genocide Convention, making it a significant precedent in international law. The court ordered Myanmar to permit those who fled the violence to return to their homes. Myanmar is currently governed by a military junta that seized power in a 2021 coup, complicating questions of enforcement and accountability. The ruling has broad implications for how international institutions may pursue accountability for state-sponsored atrocities, though the ICJ has limited enforcement mechanisms of its own.

Measles Has Officially Returned to Endemic Status in the United States

Health authorities have declared measles officially re-established in the United States, ending the country's elimination status. Regaining that status could take years and will require coordinated public health responses across communities. The United States has lost its measles elimination status, with health officials declaring the disease officially re-established in the country. Elimination status, which the US achieved in 2000, meant that any measles cases were the result of importation rather than sustained domestic transmission. That standard can no longer be met. Public health officials and researchers say restoring elimination status will likely require years of effort and will depend on raising vaccination rates in communities where coverage has declined. The return of measles is occurring in the context of a broader decline in routine childhood vaccination rates nationally since the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as reduced public trust in vaccine recommendations in some communities. Officials outline several strategies communities will need to adopt to protect people in the interim, including targeted vaccination campaigns and improved disease surveillance.
