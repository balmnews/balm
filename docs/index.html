The Supreme Court will soon take up legal challenges to the Trump administration's immigration measures, which advocates say have effectively ended asylum in the United States by combining a formal ban, mass firings, and new legal maneuvers. The Trump administration's sweeping changes to immigration policy have, in the view of immigration advocates, all but eliminated the US asylum system. The changes include an asylum ban, large-scale firings of immigration judges and adjudicators, and new legal strategies that have significantly slowed or halted processing of asylum claims. The Supreme Court is expected to consider whether some of these measures violate the due process rights of asylum seekers, a question with broad constitutional implications. The United States' obligations under domestic law and international agreements generally require that people fleeing persecution have a meaningful opportunity to present their case. Critics argue the current system no longer provides that opportunity. The administration maintains its actions are within executive authority to enforce immigration law. The Court's ruling, expected in the coming term, could either restore significant portions of the asylum process or affirm the executive's authority to restrict it.

The Supreme Court has agreed to hear a case examining whether Alaska violated the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on excessive fines when it seized a pilot's aircraft after the pilot was found carrying beer. The case centers on the constitutional limits of civil asset forfeiture, a legal mechanism that allows governments to seize property connected to alleged violations of law, sometimes without a criminal conviction. The Eighth Amendment question — whether a forfeiture is so disproportionate to the underlying offense that it constitutes an excessive fine — has gained renewed legal attention in recent years. A ruling in this case could affect how states use asset forfeiture across a range of contexts beyond aviation, with implications for property rights nationwide.

Supporters of the current structure argue the caps are necessary to prevent fraud and control costs in a program that has expanded significantly. The issue reflects broader tensions in how Medicare reimburses end-of-life care and whether the financial structure aligns with patient needs.
