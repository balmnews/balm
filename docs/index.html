Israel Election Produces No Clear Majority, Coalition Talks Begin

With nearly all ballots counted, Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud-led bloc and opposition candidate Gadi Eisenkot's alliance have each fallen short of the 61 seats needed to form a government in Israel's 120-seat Knesset, leaving coalition negotiations uncertain. Israel's national election — the first since the October 7, 2023 Hamas attack — has produced a deadlock, with no bloc securing the parliamentary majority needed to govern. With 99 percent of ballots counted, Netanyahu's Likud and its allied parties are projected to win approximately 53 seats, while the opposition alliance led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot appears positioned for roughly 48 seats. Arab parties representing Palestinian citizens of Israel performed strongly, winning an estimated 19 seats, making them potential kingmakers in any coalition scenario. Three broad paths to government formation are being discussed: Netanyahu assembles a coalition with smaller parties and possibly Arab factions; Eisenkot builds an opposition bloc that includes Arab parties and centrist defectors; or the country heads toward another round of elections if no coalition can be formed. A fourth scenario — protracted deadlock — is also considered possible. Netanyahu alleged on election night that Arab parties had committed electoral fraud, calling for a police investigation. That claim was disputed by electoral authorities and condemned by Eisenkot, who said it amounted to an attempt to delegitimize results. Qatar separately rejected Netanyahu's suggestion that it had interfered in the election, calling the accusation unfounded. The outcome is significant for US policy in the Middle East. Netanyahu's return to a governing coalition would likely mean continuity in his approach to Gaza and Iran; an Eisenkot-led government could shift those positions. The makeup of any coalition will also shape Israel's relationship with the US, and any government including Arab parties would represent a historic first.

US Economy Grew 3.4% in Third Quarter; September Inflation Rose to 3.7%

The US economy expanded at a 3.4% annual rate in the third quarter — its fastest pace in a year — driven by consumer spending and business investment in AI. Separately, the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation measure accelerated to 3.7% in September. New Commerce Department data released Thursday shows the US economy grew at an annualized rate of 3.4% in the third quarter of 2026, beating expectations and marking the strongest quarterly growth in a year. Consumer spending rose solidly, and business investment increased sharply, with analysts pointing to technology and artificial intelligence spending as a significant driver. At the same time, the Personal Consumption Expenditures index — the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge — picked up in September, reaching 3.7%. The figures arrive less than a week before midterm elections that will determine control of Congress. Despite the strong GDP number, polling has consistently shown many Americans feel negatively about the economy, with affordability concerns — particularly around grocery and housing costs — remaining widespread. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. The FDA had previously advised against the move, citing concerns about the unverified safety and efficacy of the products. The declaration bypassed the standard FDA review process. Peptide injections are marketed for a range of purposes including anti-aging, weight management, and athletic performance. Because they have not undergone clinical trials required for FDA approval, their safety profiles are not established through the standard regulatory pathway. The decision is consistent with Kennedy's broader stated approach of reducing what he describes as government overreach in medical choices, but has drawn criticism from public health officials and physicians who say it could expose patients to unverified substances.

New Study Estimates Three in Four Retired NFL Players Had CTE at Death

A new statistical study estimates that approximately three out of four retired professional football players most likely had chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, at the time of their death — a significantly higher prevalence than previously documented. A newly published study using statistical modeling techniques estimates that roughly 75 percent of retired NFL players had chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, at death. CTE is a degenerative brain disease associated with repeated head trauma, and can currently only be diagnosed post-mortem. Previous estimates of CTE prevalence have been limited by the fact that brain donation studies rely on families who self-select to donate, typically because a player showed symptoms — a process that skews results toward higher prevalence. The new study attempted to account for that selection bias using statistical methods to arrive at a population-level estimate. The finding, if it holds up to further scrutiny, would represent a substantially higher prevalence than prior estimates and has significant implications for the NFL, for athletes at younger levels of play, and for ongoing litigation and policy discussions around football safety.

Alaska Governor Orders Probe Into Democratic Senate Campaign Over Candidate Name Controversy

Alaska's Republican governor has ordered a criminal investigation into the Senate campaign of Democratic candidate Mary Peltola, after reports that her campaign was involved in recruiting a candidate sharing the name of Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan, potentially to confuse voters. Alaska Governor Mike Dunleavy announced a criminal investigation into Democratic Senate candidate Mary Peltola's campaign following reports that campaign staff had recruited a separate candidate with the same name — Dan Sullivan — as her Republican opponent, incumbent Senator Dan Sullivan. The tactic, referred to as ballot stacking, involves placing a similarly named candidate on the ballot to split or confuse votes intended for the original candidate. Peltola's campaign has not publicly confirmed involvement in recruiting the second candidate. President Trump called for criminal prosecution of Peltola in connection with the matter. Senator Lisa Murkowski, a fellow Republican, also publicly criticized Peltola's campaign, describing what she called a pattern of deception. The Alaska Senate race is one of the most closely watched in the country this cycle. Peltola, who holds the state's at-large House seat, is running to unseat Sullivan in a race Republicans consider essential to maintaining their Senate majority. Alaska uses a ranked-choice voting system, which can make ballot order and candidate names more consequential than in traditional plurality elections. The investigation is ongoing, and no charges have been filed.

High-Speed Train Collision in Spain Kills at Least 69 Near Toledo

At least 69 people were killed when a high-speed train struck a stopped train from behind near Toledo, Spain on Wednesday. Audio recordings indicate one train received clearance to move while another remained stalled ahead of it on the same track. A collision between two high-speed trains near Toledo, in central Spain, killed at least 69 people on Wednesday morning, making it one of the deadliest rail accidents in Spain in decades. Both trains had stopped after a signaling system failure. Audio released by investigators appears to show that one train was given clearance by the control center to proceed while the stopped train remained on the same track ahead of it. The train that was moving — a Renfe AVE service — struck the stationary train from behind, derailing two carriages. Dozens of passengers were injured in addition to the fatalities. Emergency services responded to the scene in Toledo province, and Spanish authorities have opened a formal investigation. The incident has raised questions about signaling protocols and communication between control centers and train operators. Spain's high-speed rail network, one of the most extensive in Europe, has maintained a strong safety record, making this collision unusual. Sources differ slightly on the precise death toll, with figures ranging from 68 to 69 across reports at the time of publication.
