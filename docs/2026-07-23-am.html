US Signs Nuclear Cooperation Agreement With Saudi Arabia, Allowing Possible Future Uranium Enrichment

The United States has signed a landmark nuclear cooperation agreement with Saudi Arabia that would give US firms access to the kingdom's civilian nuclear program and could allow Saudi Arabia to enrich uranium domestically. The deal faces congressional review and has drawn concern from Israel and nonproliferation critics. The United States and Saudi Arabia have signed a formal nuclear cooperation agreement that would provide US companies with access to Saudi Arabia's developing civilian nuclear program, potentially including the right to enrich uranium on Saudi soil. The US Department of Energy described the deal as a peaceful cooperation framework. It has been submitted to Congress for review, where it faces scrutiny from lawmakers concerned about the absence of strong monitoring safeguards. The agreement arrives at a geopolitically sensitive moment. Saudi Arabia is the world's largest crude oil exporter but has stated long-standing interest in diversifying its energy mix through nuclear power, partly to free up more oil for export. The kingdom had previously sought the right to enrich its own uranium — a condition that has historically complicated negotiations, since enrichment technology can be repurposed for weapons development. Israel, a close US ally, responded to the announcement with alarm, with officials expressing concern that the deal represents a further divergence between Washington and Jerusalem on regional security policy. Critics more broadly have warned the agreement could encourage nuclear proliferation in an already volatile Middle East, particularly given ongoing US military operations against Iran. The deal is also commercially significant for the US nuclear industry. Westinghouse, which filed for bankruptcy protection in 2017, is among the American firms positioned to benefit from expanded access to Saudi nuclear infrastructure. The agreement must clear Congress before it takes effect, and its final terms — including the specifics around enrichment rights — remain under negotiation.

US Cyclosporiasis Outbreak Surpasses 4,000 Cases, Setting Record as FDA Investigates Source

The US cyclosporiasis outbreak has surpassed 4,000 confirmed cases, making it the worst on record. The FDA is investigating the source of the Cyclospora parasite, which has proven difficult to trace because of its environmental persistence and complex transmission pathways. A cyclosporiasis outbreak in the United States has surpassed 4,000 confirmed cases, officially making it the largest such outbreak on record. The FDA is conducting an active investigation to identify the food source or sources responsible for the spread, which has been concentrated in the Midwest. Cyclosporiasis is an intestinal illness caused by ingesting food or water contaminated with the Cyclospora cayetanensis parasite. It is notoriously difficult to trace through standard food safety investigations because the parasite can be present in produce at very low levels, doesn't grow in food, and requires a laboratory-specific test for identification — tools that are not part of routine food safety testing. A recall from Taylor Farms was previously issued in connection with the outbreak, and consumer behavior has shifted noticeably, with foot traffic declining at restaurant chains associated with fresh salads and produce. Public health officials and food safety experts have said the outbreak underscores persistent gaps in the US food supply's ability to detect and respond to Cyclospora contamination. People who develop prolonged gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly diarrhea, fatigue, and loss of appetite, that do not resolve on their own are advised to consult a healthcare provider, as cyclosporiasis is treatable with antibiotics.

The ongoing Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has reached displacement camps in the conflict's epicenter, where roughly one million people are living in conditions that make infection control extremely difficult. Residents of the camps have little access to clean water, which is essential both for hygiene measures that slow transmission and for the basic care of those who fall ill. Health officials working to contain the outbreak face a particular challenge with contact tracing — the practice of identifying and monitoring all people who may have been exposed to an infected person. In active conflict zones with highly mobile, densely packed populations, this is exceptionally difficult to execute effectively. Without effective contact tracing, the virus can spread multiple generations before new cases are identified. The DRC has experienced more Ebola outbreaks than any other country, and the combination of ongoing armed conflict, mass displacement, and resource limitations has made this outbreak especially difficult to control. International health organizations are operating under severe logistical constraints. The situation has drawn comparisons to some of the most challenging outbreak environments in recent public health history.
