Supreme Court to Hear Case on Whether Retirement Fund Managers Can Consider Non-Financial Factors

The Supreme Court agreed to take up a case examining the legal boundaries around retirement fund investment decisions, including whether managers may weigh environmental, social, or other non-financial factors. The Supreme Court announced it will hear arguments in a case examining how retirement fund fiduciaries may make investment choices, specifically whether fund managers are permitted to consider factors beyond direct financial return — such as environmental, social, or governance considerations — when selecting investments. The case has broad implications for the management of pension and 401(k) funds that cover millions of American workers and retirees. Proponents of incorporating such factors argue they can reflect long-term financial risks. Critics contend that fund managers have a strict legal duty to maximize financial returns for beneficiaries and that other considerations are outside that mandate. The Court's ruling, expected in the term ahead, could clarify or reshape the standards applied to trillions of dollars in retirement assets.

Underwater Shading Structures Show Promise in Reducing Coral Bleaching

Researchers testing underwater shade structures over coral reefs in the Caribbean found the devices reduced and in some cases reversed bleaching caused by elevated sea temperatures. Scientists studying coral reef preservation have reported encouraging results from underwater shading devices designed to protect reefs from heat-driven bleaching. Testing conducted in the Caribbean found that the structures, which reduce the intensity of sunlight reaching coral, were effective at limiting bleaching and in some instances appeared to reverse existing damage. Coral bleaching occurs when sea temperatures rise above normal ranges, causing corals to expel the algae that give them color and provide essential nutrients. Prolonged bleaching typically leads to coral death. While reef restoration after mortality is possible, it is costly and labor-intensive, and restored corals remain vulnerable to future heat events. The shading approach is designed to protect existing, living reefs during heat events rather than rebuild damaged ones. Researchers cautioned that the method would need further study to assess scalability and long-term effectiveness.
