Nobel Prize in Physics Awarded to Francis Halzen for Neutrino Detection Work at South Pole

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics has been awarded to Francis Halzen, a Belgian particle physicist at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, for leading the development of the IceCube detector — a neutrino observatory built into the Antarctic ice sheet. Francis Halzen, a Belgian-born particle physicist based at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, has been awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work transforming a cubic kilometer of ice at the South Pole into a functioning detector for neutrinos. Neutrinos are subatomic particles with almost no mass that travel across the universe at near the speed of light, passing through ordinary matter with almost no interaction — making them extremely difficult to detect. The IceCube Neutrino Observatory, which Halzen helped conceive and build, uses thousands of sensors buried deep in the Antarctic ice to catch the rare moments when a neutrino collides with a water molecule, releasing a faint flash of light. The detector has allowed scientists to identify sources of high-energy neutrinos originating from outside our galaxy, opening a new observational window on the universe. IceCube has been operational since 2010 and remains the world's largest neutrino detector. The Nobel committee credited Halzen's decades of work in making the project a scientific reality.

Nobel Prize in Chemistry Awarded for Discoveries on Mirror-Image Molecules

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry has been awarded to Henri B. Kagan of Paris-Sud University and Kensō Soai of Tokyo University of Science for work showing how chemical reactions can produce an overwhelming excess of one of two mirror-image molecular forms — findings that have significantly advanced pharmaceutical manufacturing. Henri B. Kagan and Kensō Soai have been awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for independently advancing the science of asymmetric molecules — pairs of molecules that are mirror images of each other but cannot be superimposed, much like left and right hands. Many biological processes and drug compounds are sensitive to this distinction: one mirror-image form of a molecule may be therapeutically active while the other is inert or harmful. Kagan's work demonstrated how to use catalysts to drive chemical reactions toward producing predominantly one mirror-image form, a process known as asymmetric synthesis. Soai's contributions included the discovery of an autocatalytic asymmetric reaction — one in which the product itself acts as the catalyst — which helped illuminate how molecular asymmetry can be amplified. These discoveries have had lasting practical impact on the pharmaceutical industry, enabling more precise and efficient manufacturing of drugs where molecular orientation is critical to safety and effectiveness. Both scientists have been recognized as foundational figures in the field of stereoselective chemistry.

Margaret Hamilton, Whose Software Guided Apollo 11, Dies at 90

Margaret Hamilton, the computer scientist whose software powered the Apollo 11 moon landing, died on September 30 at age 90. MIT announced her death this week. Margaret Hamilton, a pioneering computer scientist whose software engineering work was instrumental in the success of the Apollo 11 mission, died on September 30 at age 90. The Massachusetts Institute of Technology announced her death this week. Hamilton led the team at MIT that developed the onboard flight software for NASA's Apollo program in the 1960s. Her work included writing the code that allowed the lunar module's computer to prioritize critical tasks during the final minutes of the moon landing — a decision credited with preventing a mission abort when alarms triggered in the descent. Working before the terms software engineer or computer scientist were in common use, Hamilton helped establish software development as a rigorous engineering discipline. She later founded her own software company and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016. Her death marks the passing of one of the foundational figures in the history of computing and space exploration.
