EPA Proposes Rule That Would Let Data Centers Obtain Air Emission Permits Without Public Notification

The Trump administration proposed a rule that would allow data centers to receive air pollution emission permits without public disclosure, removing existing notification requirements that health advocates say are a key tool for community oversight. The Environmental Protection Agency has proposed a new regulation that would allow data centers to obtain air emission permits through a process that does not require public notification. Under current rules, communities near facilities seeking emission permits are notified and given an opportunity to comment. Public health advocates say the proposed change would enable permits to be issued behind closed doors, removing a critical layer of community input. Data centers are significant sources of nitrogen oxide, which contributes to ozone formation, as well as carbon monoxide and particulate matter. The proposal comes as data center construction is expanding rapidly across the United States, driven largely by demand for artificial intelligence computing capacity. Critics of the proposal argue that communities near data centers — often lower-income areas — would lose their primary avenue for challenging pollution permits that affect local air quality.

Study Finds at Least 25% of Recently Deceased NFL Players Had Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy

A new study found that at least one in four former NFL players who died between 2016 and 2021 had CTE, the brain disease linked to repeated head trauma. Researchers said the true prevalence may be higher, and a former OSHA chief scientist said the rates were unlike anything seen in other industries. A newly published study examining former NFL players who died between 2016 and 2021 found that at least 25% had chronic traumatic encephalopathy, the degenerative brain disease associated with repeated head impacts. Researchers emphasized that this figure likely understates the true rate of the disease, as not all brains from the study period were available for examination and CTE can only be definitively diagnosed after death. The findings add to a growing body of evidence linking professional football to long-term neurological damage. A former chief scientist at the Occupational Safety and Health Administration commented that rates of occupational disease at this level are extraordinarily unusual and would typically prompt federal regulatory attention, raising questions about why OSHA has not intervened in the sport. CTE has been documented in football players for roughly two decades, but the scale of its prevalence among professional players remains contested because of the limitations of post-mortem studies. The NFL has taken steps to reduce certain types of hits in recent years, though researchers say the structural nature of the sport means head trauma remains inherent to play.

Preliminary data from roughly half of European countries shows at least 35,000 more deaths than normally expected during four record-breaking back-to-back heatwaves this summer, with Germany, France, and Spain accounting for a significant share of the toll. At least 35,000 excess deaths — fatalities above what would normally be expected — were recorded across Europe during four consecutive record-breaking heatwaves this summer, according to incomplete figures covering approximately half the continent. Germany, France, and Spain alone accounted for a large portion of the total. Because the data is partial, the actual death toll across all European countries is likely to be substantially higher. Excess mortality calculations compare observed deaths during a period to a historical baseline, providing a measure of deaths attributable to unusual conditions. Heat is now one of the leading causes of weather-related death in Europe. The pattern of repeated, intensifying heatwaves has prompted insurance rating agency S&P to separately warn that heat-related claims are beginning to affect European insurers' financial results. Public health experts have long warned that elderly populations, people with cardiovascular and respiratory conditions, and those without access to cooling are disproportionately vulnerable during extreme heat events.
